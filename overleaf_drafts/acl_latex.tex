\begin{abstract}
Practitioners often read intermediate layers of off-the-shelf encoders, check them for anisotropy with mean pairwise cosine, and correct the embedding space post hoc.
We test this practice on medieval Latin canon law, where a scholarly edition labels which manuscript copies (witnesses) share a source.
In the raw T5 encoders LaTa, PhilTa and mT5-base, 26 of 36 layers rank same-source pairs near chance, although their first and last layers retrieve well.
Mean pairwise cosine gives no reliable warning of this collapse, while the share of variance on the first principal component is high at every collapsed layer.
The signal is masked, not missing: a projection fit on training embeddings alone brings all 100 layers of our six-encoder panel to AUROC 0.962--0.987.
Four of the five raw T5-family encoders we test, these three and T5-v1.1-base, collapse, and all four share the T5 v1.1 layout; encoder-only siblings pretrained on the same corpora and the original T5-base do not.
Removing about three principal directions restores every collapsed layer, while zeroing the ten largest coordinates restores none.
After the projection the six encoders route witnesses to their source or to ``new'' about equally well, but no frozen encoder at its train-selected layer beats a character n-gram reference.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Off-the-shelf encoders are a common tool for finding reused text in digital-humanities collections \citep[e.g.,][]{manjavacas2019allusive,schelb2026locisimiles}.
Practitioners often read an intermediate layer, because intermediate layers can outperform the final one \citep{skean2025layer}.
They check the space for anisotropy, usually with the mean cosine between embeddings \citep{ethayarajh2019contextual}.
They then correct it, for example by removing the top principal components \citep{mu2018allbutthetop} or by whitening \citep{su2021whitening}.
This practice assumes that the check warns when a layer retrieves poorly.
Mean cosine is already known to be a poor isotropy measure \citep{rudman2022isoscore}; we ask whether it, or any label-free statistic, warns where retrieval fails.

Medieval Latin suits this test.
The Carolingian Canon Law project \citep[CCL;][]{firey2009ccl} keys each transcribed manuscript unit to the source it copies, with expert labels that no model shaped.
Our testbed holds 1,705 \emph{witnesses}, manuscript copies of 840 sources.
We evaluate two tasks: \emph{ranking} pairs of witnesses by cosine, scored by AUROC, and \emph{routing} each witness to its source or to ``new''.
Because witnesses copy their source's wording, a character n-gram reference ranks pairs at 0.987 AUROC, so a retrieval failure points to the representation.
Latin also offers T5 and encoder-only models from the same authors and pretraining corpora.

Three raw T5 encoders, trained with no embedding objective, fail at mid-depth (Figure~\ref{fig:depth}).
In LaTa, PhilTa and mT5-base \citep{xue2021mt5}, 26 of 36 layers rank same-source pairs at AUROC 0.50 to 0.66, near chance.
Their first and last layers still retrieve, and the three embedding-trained encoders of our panel never collapse.
The usual check, mean pairwise cosine, gives no reliable warning in either direction.
The share of variance on the first principal component (top-PC share) is high at every collapsed layer: necessary for collapse in our models, but not sufficient.

The signal is masked, not missing.
All-but-the-Top \citep[ABTT;][]{mu2018allbutthetop}, fit on training embeddings only, removes the mean and the top principal components from every embedding, with no parameter update.
It brings all 100 layers of the six panel models to AUROC 0.962 to 0.987, the 26 collapsed layers included.
The projection is linear and sees no test label, so the ranking it recovers is already present in each collapsed layer.

Controls show which models collapse.
The encoder-only siblings LaBERTa and PhilBERTa never fall below 0.826 and 0.883.
Within the T5 family, T5-v1.1-base collapses on the same Latin text (lowest AUROC 0.489), while the original T5-base \citep{raffel2020t5} never falls below 0.816.
Four of the five raw T5-family encoders we test collapse, and all four share the T5 v1.1 layout: a gated-GELU feed-forward block \citep{shazeer2020glu} and untied input and output embeddings \citep{t5v11release}.
We do not isolate which change is responsible, and T5-base also saw supervised tasks in pretraining, which this contrast does not separate from layout.

Inside the collapsed layers, about three directions mask the signal.
Removing three principal components brings all 26 layers to AUROC 0.914 or above, and mT5-base keeps improving up to ten.
Zeroing the ten largest coordinates restores none of them, although the first principal component (the dominant direction) sits mostly on such coordinates.
In LaTa, pooling without the comma, the period and \texttt{</s>} restores every collapsed layer; in mT5-base, no set of up to 100 token types does.

This paper makes four contributions.
(1)~The first per-layer retrieval profile of T5 encoder states that we know of, each layer read on its own rather than averaged with another layer (Section~\ref{sec:depth}).
(2)~A within-family contrast: T5-v1.1-base collapses on the same text where the original T5-base does not, and every collapsing model has the T5 v1.1 layout; encoder-only siblings on the same corpora do not collapse, and the high-share middle layers also appear on English text (Section~\ref{sec:models}).
(3)~A localization of the mask to about three principal directions, which zeroing the ten largest coordinates does not remove, with a token-level account in LaTa and none in mT5-base (Section~\ref{sec:localizing}).
(4)~A measure of what the projection gains for routing and where it stops, set against a character n-gram reference (Section~\ref{sec:routing}).
We will release the frozen testbed, split, evaluation code and character n-gram reference scores.
We localize and repair the collapse but do not explain why the T5 v1.1 layout produces it.

\section{Testbed and Protocol}
\label{sec:testbed}

\paragraph{Corpus and labels.}
\label{sec:dataset}
The testbed comes from CCL \citep{firey2009ccl}, an open-access edition of early medieval, mostly ninth-century, canon-law manuscripts.
A \emph{witness} is one manuscript's copy of a source text, kept as one file.
Witnesses that copy the same source share a \emph{directory}; the testbed holds 1,705 witnesses in 840 directories.
The labels are expert-assigned by the project and checked by collation in its parallel display (Appendix~\ref{app:corpus_provenance}).

\paragraph{Split and train-only rule.}
\label{sec:setup}
\label{sec:layers}
We split the witnesses into 847 training and 858 test witnesses, with 565 and 596 positive pairs; pairs form only within a split (Appendix~\ref{app:testbed_details}).
Of the test witnesses, 535 have a same-source partner in test (\emph{existing}) and 323 do not (\emph{new}).
The layer, the routing threshold and the post-hoc correction are all chosen or fit on the training split.
Each headline number (Table~\ref{tab:headline}) reads a model at its layer of highest training AUROC for ranking, or of highest training routing accuracy (DirAcc@1, defined below) for routing (Table~\ref{tab:selected_layers}).
The test split selects nothing.

\paragraph{Tasks and metrics.}
\label{sec:tasks}
\emph{Ranking} scores every pair of test witnesses by the cosine of their mean-pooled embeddings.
We report AUROC with same-source pairs as positives.
\emph{Routing} is open-set: each test witness goes to an existing directory or to ``new''.
Each test directory scores a witness by the highest cosine between the witness and any member.
A pseudo-directory \texttt{NEW} scores a fixed threshold $\tau$, chosen to maximize training F1 at separating same-source from different-source pairs.
The witness goes to the highest-scoring directory.
DirAcc@1 counts a decision as correct if it names the labeled directory, or \texttt{NEW} for a new witness.
Assignment accuracy scores only the existing-versus-new decision; always answering existing scores 62.4\%.

A \emph{collapsed layer} has baseline test AUROC below 0.70.
The cutoff only describes layers; it selects no reported number.
Two label-free statistics, computed on the training embeddings, describe each layer.
The \emph{mean pairwise cosine} is the usual anisotropy check \citep{ethayarajh2019contextual}.
The \emph{top-PC share} is the share of variance on the first principal component of the centered embeddings.

\input{tables/models_main}

\paragraph{Models.}
Of the ten models in Table~\ref{tab:models}, six form the \emph{panel}, which carries the full analysis over 100 layers.
Three are raw T5 encoders with no embedding objective: the Latin LaTa \citep{lata2023card}, its Ancient Greek, Latin and English sibling PhilTa \citep{riemenschneider2023lata}, and mT5-base \citep{xue2021mt5}.
Three are embedding-trained: LaBSE \citep{feng2022labse}, Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B \citep[Qwen3-0.6B;][]{qwen3embedding2025} and KaLM-mini \citep{kalm2025embedding}.
Four raw controls complete the main text, two of them the encoder-only siblings LaBERTa and PhilBERTa.
LaTa and LaBERTa, and PhilTa and PhilBERTa, share authors and pretraining corpora \citep{lata2023card,laberta2023card,riemenschneider2023lata}.
The other two are English T5 encoders that share a tokenizer: T5-v1.1-base \citep{t5v11release} and the original T5-base \citep{raffel2020t5}.
We say a model has the \emph{T5 v1.1 layout} when it has a gated-GELU feed-forward \citep{shazeer2020glu} and untied input and output embeddings \citep{t5v11release}.
LaTa, PhilTa, mT5-base and T5-v1.1-base have it; T5-base keeps a ReLU feed-forward and tied embeddings.
We mean-pool the hidden states of every transformer block, dropping only padding and tokens that decode to no text, such as SentencePiece's bare word-boundary piece; special tokens such as \texttt{</s>} and punctuation are kept.
Mean pooling routes better than last-token pooling in all five models we checked (Appendix~\ref{app:lasttok}).
Sentence-T5 \citep{ni2022sentencet5} and SPhilBERTa \citep{riemenschneider2023sphilberta} appear only in Table~\ref{tab:all_models}.

\paragraph{Post-processing and lexical reference.}
\label{sec:postproc}
ABTT \citep{mu2018allbutthetop} subtracts the training mean and removes the top $D$ principal components of the centered training embeddings from every vector.
We choose $D \in \{1,2,3,5,7,10\}$ per layer by training DirAcc@1.
ABTT is fit on training embeddings only and updates no model parameter; we also call it \emph{the projection}.
SIF frequency weighting \citep{arora2017sif} and reduced-rank whitening \citep{su2021whitening} are baselines in Appendix~\ref{app:sif_variants}.
The task is lexically solvable by design, because the witnesses of one source copy its wording.
Our \emph{character n-gram reference}, TF-IDF over character 3--5-grams fit on training witnesses, reaches 0.987 AUROC and 89.9 DirAcc@1 (Table~\ref{tab:lexical_baselines}), the level the projection approaches.

\section{Collapse by Depth}
\label{sec:depth}

This section asks whether the middle layers of our encoders retrieve, and whether the usual anisotropy check warns when they do not.
In three of the six panel models most middle layers rank near chance, and a projection fit on training embeddings restores them all: the signal is masked, not missing.

\paragraph{LaTa, PhilTa and mT5-base fall to near chance at mid-depth.}
Figure~\ref{fig:depth} gives baseline ranking AUROC at every layer of the six panel models.
In LaTa, PhilTa and mT5-base, 26 of the 36 layers collapse, ranking at AUROC 0.50 to 0.66.
They are layers 2--11 of LaTa, 3--11 of PhilTa and 5--11 of mT5-base.
The next-lowest layer in the panel ranks at 0.799 (mT5-base, layer~4), so in the panel any cutoff from 0.66 to 0.79 gives the same split.
A cutoff on training AUROC picks the same 26 layers, so no test score defines the collapse.
The first and last layers still retrieve: LaTa ranks at 0.934 at layer~1 and 0.938 at layer~12.
The embedding-trained models stay at or above 0.806 at every layer.

\paragraph{A train-only projection restores ranking at every layer of the six panel models.}
ABTT (Section~\ref{sec:testbed}) lifts all 100 layers of the panel, the 26 collapsed layers included, to AUROC 0.962 to 0.987 (Figure~\ref{fig:depth}, solid lines).
This bears out the reading of \citet{timkey2021rogue}, that a few dominant dimensions hide representational quality, here at layers that rank near chance.
Reduced-rank whitening, fit on the same training embeddings, ranks within 0.021 of ABTT at every layer (Table~\ref{tab:whiten_reduced}, Appendix~\ref{app:sif_variants}).
The restoration holds for ranking only: routing after ABTT still varies by layer, from 75.6 to 90.2 DirAcc@1 (Appendix~\ref{app:per_layer}).

\paragraph{Mean pairwise cosine gives no reliable warning.}
Figure~\ref{fig:diagnostics}a plots mean pairwise cosine, computed on training embeddings, against baseline test AUROC.
The collapsed layers of LaTa, PhilTa and mT5-base have a lower mean cosine (0.22 to 0.58) than the embedding-trained models, which retrieve well (0.59 to 0.97).
T5-v1.1-base, which also collapses on the Latin text (Table~\ref{tab:models}), sits at a high mean cosine (0.87 to 0.96).
So neither a high nor a low mean cosine marks a collapsed layer.

\paragraph{Top-PC share is high at every collapsed layer.}
Top-PC share needs no labels either (Figure~\ref{fig:diagnostics}b).
It is at least 0.76 at every collapsed layer of the panel and at every collapsed layer of T5-v1.1-base.
In the three embedding-trained panel models it peaks at 0.524 (LaBSE).
A high share is necessary for collapse in our models, but it is not sufficient.
mT5-base layer~4 has a share of 0.893 and still ranks at 0.799.
Sentence-T5 \citep{ni2022sentencet5}, an embedding-trained T5 encoder reported in the appendix, combines shares of 0.66 to 0.94 with AUROC 0.800 to 0.891 at layers 2--11 (Table~\ref{tab:all_models}).
The share marks the collapsed regime but does not order layers inside it (Appendix~\ref{app:depth_extra}).

\section{Collapse Across Models}
\label{sec:models}

This section asks which models collapse and what they share.
Of the ten models in Table~\ref{tab:models}, four collapse: LaTa, PhilTa, mT5-base and T5-v1.1-base.
All four have the T5 v1.1 layout, and none of the other six collapses at any layer.

\paragraph{Encoder-only siblings pretrained on the same corpora do not collapse.}
LaBERTa and PhilBERTa have no embedding objective.
The T5 members collapse, with lowest AUROC 0.496 (LaTa) and 0.538 (PhilTa).
The siblings never fall below 0.826 (LaBERTa) and 0.883 (PhilBERTa).
Their top-PC share also stays low, peaking at 0.254 and 0.162 against 0.952 and 0.858 in LaTa and PhilTa.
Shared pretraining text and a missing embedding objective therefore do not produce the collapse on their own.
Architecture, pretraining objective and tokenizer vary together within each pair, so the pairs cannot say which of them matters (Appendix~\ref{app:models}).

\paragraph{T5-v1.1-base collapses; the original T5-base does not.}
The two models share their tokenizer, the span-corruption objective and the C4 pretraining text \citep{raffel2020t5,t5v11release}.
On the Latin corpus, T5-v1.1-base falls to 0.489 at layer 2, and its layers 2 to 11 collapse.
T5-base stays at 0.816 or above at every layer, and its top-PC share peaks at 0.545.
The two differ in layout (Section~\ref{sec:testbed}), and by their checkpoint configurations LaTa, PhilTa and mT5-base share T5-v1.1-base's (Table~\ref{tab:all_models}).
Across the twelve models we test, the collapse tracks the T5 v1.1 layout; we do not isolate which change is responsible.
T5-base also saw supervised tasks in pretraining \citep{raffel2020t5}, so this contrast does not separate layout from pretraining mix.
We did not apply the projection to the controls (Limitations).

\paragraph{In three T5 v1.1-layout models, the high-share middle layers appear on English text too.}
T5-v1.1-base was pretrained on English alone, so its collapse on Latin could be an out-of-distribution effect.
We read mT5-base, PhilTa and T5-v1.1-base on 847 English passages of US court opinions \citep{cap2024,kandpal2025commonpile}, paired one to one with the Latin training witnesses and matched in length.
In all three models, layers 4 to 11 keep a top-PC share of at least 0.82 on English, and the first and last layers stay at or below 0.34 (Table~\ref{tab:gen_geometry} and Figure~\ref{fig:gen_geometry}).
At layers 2 to 11 of T5-v1.1-base, top-PC share is 0.931 to 0.950 on English and 0.958 to 0.975 on Latin.
In these models, then, the high-share middle layers do not depend on Latin input.
The check covers geometry only: without labels, it says nothing about retrieval on English.

\paragraph{In LaTa, contrastive fine-tuning leaves the middle layers collapsed.}
We fine-tuned LaTa contrastively on 499 training pairs (Appendix~\ref{app:reference_systems}).
Fine-tuning lifts the last layer from 0.938 to 0.984 AUROC.
Layers 2 to 11 stay at 0.50 to 0.57.
Their geometry barely moves: peak top-PC share is 0.945 after fine-tuning, against 0.952 before (Table~\ref{tab:ft_lata_layerwise}).
This is consistent with \citet{haemmerl2023anisotropy}, who report that a sentence-tuned multilingual encoder has no large outlier dimension at its output layer but still has one at a middle layer.

\section{Localizing the Collapse}
\label{sec:localizing}

This section asks what masks the retrieval signal in the collapsed layers and where the mask sits.
The mask is about three directions; zeroing the ten largest coordinates does not remove it, and only in LaTa do a few token types feed it.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_localize}
\caption{Test ranking AUROC at the 26 collapsed layers of LaTa (10 layers), PhilTa (9) and mT5-base (7); lines: median over a model's collapsed layers; bands: their range. (a)~Zeroing the $k$ coordinates of largest mean absolute value; dotted: $k$ random coordinates (mean of five draws), within 0.001 of the baseline up to $k=10$ and within 0.03 at 400. Up to $k=10$ (vertical line) the best layer reaches 0.794. (b)~Removing the top $D$ principal components ($D=0$: centering only). Dashed line: AUROC 0.90. All fits use training embeddings.}
\label{fig:localize}
\end{figure*}

We run each test at the 26 collapsed layers, fit every intervention on training embeddings, and read test ranking AUROC; a layer counts as restored at 0.90.
We fixed most tests before running them ($\dagger$, Table~\ref{tab:predictions}); four of these fail.
Appendix~\ref{app:localizing} gives per-layer tables and further controls.

\paragraph{Zeroing the ten largest coordinates restores no collapsed layer.}
In pretrained transformers a few outlier coordinates dominate cosine similarity \citep{timkey2021rogue}.
Zeroing them improves pooled similarity in pretrained encoders \citep{luo2021positional,haemmerl2023anisotropy}.
We therefore expected that zeroing five or fewer coordinates of largest variance on training embeddings would restore the collapsed layers.
Zeroing up to ten, ranked by variance or by mean absolute value, restores none of the 26, and the best layer reaches 0.794 (Figure~\ref{fig:localize}a).
The dominant direction does load mostly on these coordinates: the ten of highest variance carry a median 92\% of its squared loading.
Yet zeroing them leaves the direction dominant: on the remaining coordinates it still holds a median 72\% of the variance and still masks retrieval.
Zeroing far more coordinates does restore LaTa and PhilTa: ranked by variance, the first layers recover at 50 coordinates and all 19 by 300 (by 400 under the mean-absolute-value ranking of Figure~\ref{fig:localize}a); no mT5-base layer recovers even at 400.
This extends the outlier-dimension account: the masking direction concentrates on a few coordinates yet acts beyond them.

\paragraph{Removing three directions restores every collapsed layer.}
ABTT removes whole directions instead of coordinates, so we vary the number $D$ of principal components it removes (Figure~\ref{fig:localize}b).
Centering alone ($D=0$) does not help, as expected: the median AUROC over the collapsed layers falls from 0.541 to 0.503.
At these layers the first component holds a median 94\% of the centered training variance.
We expected that removing it alone would recover at least 80\% of the gain from removing ten.
It recovers a median 45\%, and reaches 80\% at only one layer of 26.
Three components suffice for 0.91: they bring all 26 layers to 0.914 or above.
mT5-base keeps improving up to ten components (median 0.934 at three, 0.978 at ten).
Ranking with the ten removed components alone gives a median AUROC of 0.489, against 0.977 for the retained subspace: at these layers the mask occupies a few directions and the signal lies outside them.

\paragraph{In LaTa, three token types feed the removed directions.}
A pooled vector is the mean of its token vectors, so a few token types with large values along the removed directions would set a witness's position on them.
We ranked token types on training witnesses by how much of the variance of the witness scores on the top three components they contribute, then pooled without the top $m$ types, for $m$ up to 100.
We expected some $m$ to restore the collapsed layers.
In LaTa, $m=3$ does.
Pooling without the comma, the period and \texttt{</s>} drops 11\% of the test tokens and restores all 10 collapsed layers (median 0.919).
Dropping random token types of the same training mass leaves the median at 0.500.
The dominant direction also does not track passage length: its score correlates with log token count at $|\rho| \le 0.44$ on test passages at every collapsed layer.
The tokens are carriers, not a cause: LaBERTa also pools commas, periods and \texttt{</s>}, and it does not collapse (Section~\ref{sec:models}).
PhilTa needs more types: dropping its top 30, about a third of its tokens, restores all 9 of its collapsed layers on test passages (7 of 9 on training passages).

\paragraph{In mT5-base, no set of up to 100 token types restores a collapsed layer.}
Dropping up to 100 token types, ranked the same way, restores none of its 7 collapsed layers; the best reaches 0.748.
mT5-base also resists coordinate zeroing, yet removing three to ten directions repairs it.
Its mask is thus confined neither to a few coordinates nor to a few token types, and we found no token-level account.

\section{Routing and Limits}
\label{sec:routing}

This section asks what the projection gains for routing and where it stops.
At their train-selected layers after ABTT, the six frozen encoders route about equally well, but none beats the n-gram reference.

\input{tables/headline_main}

\paragraph{At train-selected layers, routing after the projection converges across the six encoders.}
After ABTT, the six encoders route within 3.3 DirAcc@1 points of each other (95\% CI 1.8 to 6.0), from 86.1 to 89.4, at the train-selected layers of Table~\ref{tab:headline}.
Without correction they span 39.3 points (34.6 to 43.7), from 46.6 for mT5-base to 85.9 for KaLM-mini.
Every encoder's routing gain has a bootstrap interval that excludes zero, from 3.5 points for KaLM-mini (1.0 to 6.0) to 41.8 for mT5-base (Table~\ref{tab:headline_ci_diffs}, Appendix~\ref{app:ci_pq}).

\paragraph{For the embedding-trained encoders, the routing gain does not depend on the threshold.}
LaBSE, Qwen3-0.6B and KaLM-mini already rank well without correction (AUROC 0.956 to 0.972), so their routing gain cannot come from a ranking repair.
ABTT still raises their DirAcc@1 by 7.0, 9.1 and 3.5 points.
Because routing applies a threshold $\tau$ fit on training pairs, we also score the existing-versus-new decision without it, as the AUROC of each test witness's best-match cosine, existing against new.
ABTT raises it by 0.037 for LaBSE, 0.055 for Qwen3-0.6B and 0.027 for KaLM-mini, and every paired interval excludes zero (Appendix~\ref{app:routing_no_threshold}; per-cell values in Table~\ref{tab:pq_routing}).
The assignment-accuracy gain also holds at each setting's best test threshold and under a finer threshold grid (Appendix~\ref{app:ci_pq}).
For these three encoders, centering alone ($D=0$) scores 12 to 26 points lower in assignment accuracy than ABTT at the same layer (Table~\ref{tab:headline_ci_diffs}).
We do not test what in the removed components produces the routing gain.

\paragraph{No frozen encoder at its train-selected layer beats a character n-gram reference.}
The best projected cells of Table~\ref{tab:headline} tie the reference of Section~\ref{sec:testbed}: LaBSE ranks at 0.987, and Qwen3-0.6B and KaLM-mini route at 89.4, with every difference interval including zero.
Two ranking cells fall below it with intervals that exclude zero, LaTa and Qwen3-0.6B (by 0.016 and 0.014 AUROC); mT5-base falls 0.012 below, with an interval that touches zero (0.000 to 0.027).
LaTa's routing cell falls 3.7 points below it (Table~\ref{tab:headline_ci_diffs}).
Our claims therefore concern where the retrieval signal sits inside the encoders and what masks it.

\paragraph{Fine-tuning Qwen3-0.6B and KaLM-mini adds about two routing points over the projection.}
As a supervised reference, we fine-tune LaTa, Qwen3-0.6B and KaLM-mini contrastively on 499 training pairs (Appendix~\ref{app:reference_systems}).
These references are flattered: 206 of the 535 existing test witnesses (38.5\%) sit in a directory that supplied a training pair.
Fine-tuned Qwen3-0.6B and KaLM-mini rank above the character n-gram reference in point estimate (0.996 and 0.997 AUROC; Table~\ref{tab:headline}).
After fine-tuning, the projection no longer helps ranking: AUROC falls slightly in point estimate in all three fine-tuned models (by 0.002 to 0.014; every interval includes zero), in line with \citet{rajaee2021finetuning}.
For routing, fine-tuning plus ABTT beats the zero-shot projection by 1.8 points for Qwen3-0.6B (92.3 against 90.5) and by 2.1 for KaLM-mini (93.0 against 90.9); on LaTa the two tie at 87.7.
These are means over five reseedings of the query and reference witnesses (Table~\ref{tab:finetune_ceiling}, Appendix~\ref{app:reference_systems}); Table~\ref{tab:headline} shows the single-split cells, where the gains are 2.0 and 3.1 points (only KaLM-mini's interval excludes zero) and LaTa falls 0.9 points.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Before reading an intermediate layer of a frozen encoder, fit a projection on training embeddings: in our panel it was cheap insurance.
In the six panel models it scored above the baseline in point estimate at all 100 layers in ranking and routing (Appendix~\ref{app:per_layer}), but routing after it still varies by layer, so the layer still matters.
For raw states, check top-PC share, not mean pairwise cosine.
In our models mean cosine gave no reliable warning in either direction, while every collapsed layer of the pretrained models had a top-PC share of at least 0.76 (Section~\ref{sec:depth}).
The 0.76 is the lowest share we observed at a collapsed layer of a pretrained checkpoint, not a cutoff validated on held-out models.
The share is a screen, not a verdict: a high share is necessary for collapse here but not sufficient, and it does not order the layers inside the collapsed regime.
Choosing among layers still needs a train-only task signal, such as training AUROC.

In decoder language models, gated feed-forward blocks are reported to amplify outlier activations \citep{fishman2025fp8,yang2025activationspikes}, but the original T5 also carries large outliers \citep{zhao2025outlier}; why the T5 v1.1 layout collapses remains open.
Our localization names carriers in LaTa, not a cause.
Concurrently, \citet{mikkelsen2026clinical} reports on clinical text a mid-depth retrieval trough in retrieval-trained encoders, with recovery in the last layers, and only partial recovery in encoders without a retrieval objective.
That pattern differs from ours: in our models neither the embedding-trained encoders nor the encoder-only siblings collapse, and that study tests no T5 model.

For the editor of a text collection, the projection makes the choice of encoder matter little (Section~\ref{sec:routing}; maps in Appendix~\ref{app:cluster_geometry}).
The existing-versus-new decision is still wrong for about one test witness in ten (assignment accuracy 88.5 to 91.7; Table~\ref{tab:headline_ci}), so the final call stays with the scholar.
Because the task is lexically solvable, a practical tool can start from character n-grams; dense encoders are reported to help where reuse is paraphrastic \citep{delaselle2026transcription}, a case our testbed does not measure.

\section*{Limitations}

Four factors are uncontrolled.
(i)~We use one labeled corpus in one language and genre, early medieval Latin canon law; its 1,705 witnesses are many for this field but few by current NLP standards.
(ii)~Within each matched pair, architecture, pretraining objective and tokenizer vary together, so the pairs do not say which of them matters; that LaTa and LaBERTa share authors and a pretraining corpus rests on their model cards \citep{lata2023card,laberta2023card}.
(iii)~The T5 v1.1 changes (gated-GELU feed-forward, untied embeddings) vary together, and T5-base also saw supervised tasks in pretraining, so our models do not separate layout from pretraining mix.
(iv)~The token ablation accounts for the masking directions in LaTa only; in mT5-base no set of up to 100 token types restores a collapsed layer, so its mask has no token-level account.

The scope of three results is narrow.
We claim the projection result for the six-model panel only, since we did not apply the projection to the four controls.
The English check measures geometry only; we have no labels for English retrieval.
The task is lexically solvable by design, and a character n-gram reference ties the best projected encoders, so we do not claim that encoders beat surface overlap on this corpus.

The fine-tuning test is small (499 pairs, at most eight epochs), and its references are flattered by directory overlap with the training pairs (Section~\ref{sec:routing}).
Our intervals resample test directories with the layer, threshold and projection held fixed, so they cover test sampling and not the choice of split.
ABTT needs enough text to estimate principal components, so smaller archives need separate study.

The labels are expert-assigned, but by one project.
Its director or supervised graduate students assigned the keys, and verification is by collation in the project's parallel display, not by independent double annotation, so no inter-annotator agreement figure exists for this testbed.
Collation compares the witnesses of one key, so it has nothing to compare when a key has a single witness, and 545 of the 840 directories hold one witness in this testbed.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}

The testbed holds early medieval legal texts, transcribed from manuscripts and early printings, and contains no personal data.
The Carolingian Canon Law project publishes its material under a Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial-ShareAlike 4.0 license \citep{ccl2026about}.
The project has confirmed in writing that the license covers the transcriptions, annotations and markup in the exports we use and that, in the view of the project's Advisory Board, attribution to the project suffices; the project's interface credits the transcriber and proofreader of each unit.
The project's corpus keeps growing and being corrected, so the testbed is a dated snapshot (version 1), fixed by a sha256 manifest of its 1,705 labeled files, and every number in this paper comes from it.
We will release it under the same license, in coordination with the project's periodic versioned exports, and later versions will follow those exports.
The intended use is research on retrieval and on assisted source identification: the system ranks candidates for an expert to judge and does not identify sources on its own.
The English control sample consists of public-domain US court opinions from the Caselaw Access Project \citep{cap2024}, which name litigants; we use it only for label-free geometry statistics and do not release it.
We used AI assistants for writing and checking code; the authors verified all code and numbers.

\section{Corpus Provenance and Derivation}
\label{app:corpus_provenance}

This appendix records where the testbed text comes from and how our files were derived from it, in the detail that Section~\ref{sec:dataset} leaves out.

\paragraph{The project.} CCL \citep{firey2009ccl} is an open-access digital edition founded in 2009; \citet{eichbauer2014ccl} reviews it.\footnote{Founding year, pre-2019 workflow, proofreading standard, the one manuscript transcribed by handwritten text recognition (HTR), the design of the identifiers, the assignment and verification of keys, and the extent of unidentified material: unpublished documentation and correspondence supplied by the project, 2026.} Its texts are early medieval canon-law manuscripts, most of them ninth-century and never edited to modern standards. Each manuscript is identified by its siglum (Place-Library-Shelfmark), and a witness is one manuscript's copy of a source text, transcribed as one unit (one canon or excerpt).

\paragraph{Transcription.} Transcription is manual, from manuscript images or, for some texts, eighteenth-century printings, with one exception: HTR has been attempted on one manuscript (Paris, BnF, lat.\ 1454; 142 of the 1,705 witnesses), and its transcription is still in proofreading. Before 2019 transcribers worked from purchased microfilm into word-processed files, which CCL contributors converted to TEI-P5 (the Text Encoding Initiative's XML standard for scholarly editions). Since 2019 they transcribe in the CCL Transcription Desk beside a IIIF image of the folio (a zoomable web-served scan of the manuscript leaf); an approved proofreader on the Proofreading Desk then checks the text against the image to the letter and adds TEI markup \citep{ccl2026desks}. That proofreading stage belongs to the post-2019 workflow. The interface credits the transcriber and proofreader of each unit \citep{ccl2026about}.

\paragraph{Identifiers.} The project has two identifier systems, both designed at its launch in 2009 by its director in consultation with a mathematician, a software developer and a second scholar of early medieval canon law. The first is a location identifier. Every manuscript is a unique object, so every unit has exactly one place in one manuscript, and the identifier records it as the manuscript siglum, the folium, and the unit's position on that folium. Our file names are these identifiers. The second is the source key. Many units in different manuscripts copy the same prescription issued by the same authority, and the key names that common source. It encodes the type of source (a church council, a papal decretal, a royal capitulary, a theologian, a penitential), the point of origin (a particular council, pope or author), the date of origin, and the item's reference in modern scholarship, such as a canon, letter or register number. The key is designed so that a researcher can find the modern authoritative edition of the source where one exists.

\paragraph{Labels.} CCL keys a unit to its original source, such as a fourth-century council or a papal decretal, once that source is identified, and our directories reuse that key as supplied. In a label, a four-letter code carries the type of source and its point of origin, followed by the year and the canon or chapter number, as in \texttt{CTOU.567.16}; some labels carry a trailing annotation, as in \texttt{CTOU.567.16 (15)} or \texttt{CARL.501?.18}. Counting a directory as key-named when its label begins with a four-letter code, a period, a year and a period, whatever follows, 689 of the 840 directory labels are source keys. The other 151 are modern edition citations, the Apostolic Canons (\texttt{Can.apost.}$N$), references to other collections such as the Capitula Martini, biblical references, and a handful of unidentified sources; each unidentified source is a singleton directory and contributes no positive pairs.

\paragraph{Assignment and verification.} Keys were assigned by the project's director, a specialist in early medieval canon law, or by graduate students working under the director's supervision, who had enough Latin to check a manuscript version against the modern authoritative version of the source. Verification, and occasional correction, runs through the project's parallel display, which sets side by side all units that share a key and shows how far their wording agrees. A misidentified unit stands out there as a text that cannot be a version of the others. This is verification by collation (see Limitations). The project supplied two label corrections during this study, and both are logged with the frozen testbed. We assigned no label ourselves and changed only those two. What remains unidentified is large: across the several hundred manuscripts that contain the project's texts, thousands of units still carry no key, and they are the queries that routing is meant to serve.

\paragraph{Derivation.} We hold the witnesses as plain text derived from CCL TEI-P5 exports. The rule is one file per unit: the directory is the source key from the unit's \texttt{scholSource} division, which is stripped from the text so that no file carries its own label; the text is the manuscript rubric followed by the canon text, with inline markup flattened (editorial notes kept, deletions dropped) and layout whitespace collapsed; a unit without a source key receives no directory. The rule is scripted; checked against a current export of one manuscript (Paris, BnF, lat.\ 2123), it reproduces all 279 units present in both the export and the corpus, 100 percent after whitespace normalization with no character-level disagreement, and places every one of them in the same directory.

\section{Testbed and Protocol Details}
\label{app:testbed_details}

This appendix gives the parts of the protocol that Section~\ref{sec:testbed} leaves out: how the split treats directories of each size, the further layer statistics, the SIF weighting, and how the lexical reference fares by degree of overlap.

\paragraph{Split.}
Singleton directories (545 of 840) are divided file by file between the two splits.
Doubletons go whole to one side.
Larger directories are divided internally, so that every size class yields training positives.
Pairs form only within a split.

\paragraph{Further layer statistics.}
Let $p_i = \lambda_i / \sum_j \lambda_j$ for the covariance eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \dots$ of the centered training embeddings; the top-PC share is $p_1$.
The \emph{effective rank} is $\exp(-\sum_i p_i \log p_i)$, the entropy effective rank of \citet{roy2007effectiverank} applied to the covariance eigenvalues.
It is 1 when one direction holds all the variance.
The \emph{cosine gap} $g$ is the mean same-directory test cosine minus the mean different-directory test cosine.
The \emph{standardized gap} $g/s$ divides it by the standard deviation $s$ of all pairwise test cosines, which puts models with different cosine spreads on one scale.
The two gaps need labels; the effective rank, like the top-PC share and the mean pairwise cosine, does not.
Our mean pairwise cosine averages over all pairs of pooled passages, an adaptation of the word-level statistic of \citet{ethayarajh2019contextual}, which uses random pairs.

\paragraph{SIF.}
SIF \citep{arora2017sif} reweights each token by $a/(a+p(w))$ before pooling, with $a=10^{-3}$ and $p(w)$ estimated on training texts.
It gives special tokens, such as the end-of-sequence token \texttt{</s>}, zero weight.
We use only this weighting step of SIF, not its common-component removal.
SIF+ABTT applies both corrections, and ABTT-only names ABTT when we contrast the two.

\paragraph{Software.}
We extract hidden states with PyTorch \citep{paszke2019pytorch} and Hugging Face Transformers \citep{wolf2020transformers}, fit whitening and the character TF-IDF reference with scikit-learn \citep{pedregosa2011scikit}, and compute principal components and the remaining statistics with NumPy \citep{harris2020numpy}.
BM25 is our own implementation.

\paragraph{Lexical reference by overlap.}
We cut the positive test pairs into terciles of n-gram overlap, using the TF-IDF score itself.
In every tercile, the character n-gram reference ranks a witness's partner first at least as often as every baseline and ABTT encoder configuration at its train-selected layer.
Table~\ref{tab:lexical_baselines} gives all three lexical scorers.

\section{Bootstrap Intervals and Routing Checks}
\label{app:ci_pq}

This appendix gives the intervals behind Table~\ref{tab:headline} and the routing checks that Section~\ref{sec:routing} quotes.

\paragraph{Intervals.} A bootstrap replicate draws the 514 test directories with replacement ($B=10{,}000$, seed 233). A drawn directory keeps every file and pair: a directory drawn $c$ times weights its files and within-directory pairs by $c$ and its pairs with a directory drawn $c'$ times by $cc'$, and no pair is formed between two copies of one directory. The layer, threshold, ABTT components, $D$, and SIF weights stay fit on the training split, and every score is computed once on the full test set, so a replicate only reweights test pairs and files. Intervals are 95\% percentiles; a paired difference recomputes both cells on each replicate. Before resampling, every cell was recomputed with the paper's evaluation code and matched its printed value. Table~\ref{tab:headline_ci} gives every cell and Table~\ref{tab:headline_ci_diffs} the contrasts quoted in Section~\ref{sec:routing}.

\paragraph{Routing checks.} Table~\ref{tab:pq_routing} reports, at the headline routing cells, the AUROC of each test witness's maximum cosine for existing against new witnesses (no threshold), the gap between the best test threshold and the training threshold, the skewness of the 10-occurrence distribution \citep{radovanovic2010hubs}, centering alone ($D=0$), and assignment accuracy with $\tau$ refit as the exact best-F1 cut over all training pair scores.

\paragraph{Threshold grid.} We keep the grid of 200 evenly spaced thresholds in $[0,1]$ used for every printed number. Every finer grid optimizes the same training objective, the best F1 over same- against different-directory training pairs, and differs only in resolution. With the layer and $D$ fixed, a grid of 10,001 values moves 45 of the 66 printed routing numbers, by a median of 3 files (0.35 points); ABTT, SIF+ABTT, and fine-tuned cells move by at most 0.8 points, and Base and SIF cells by up to 2.6 (mT5-base's baseline). The exact best-F1 cut moves 48 of them. Refitting would trade one set of differences of one to a few files for another and changes no conclusion of Section~\ref{sec:routing}.

\section{Routing Gain Without a Threshold}
\label{app:routing_no_threshold}

This appendix gives the paired contrasts behind the second finding of Section~\ref{sec:routing}, for the three embedding-trained encoders.
Each contrast compares the ABTT cell with the baseline cell, each at its own train-selected routing layer, and its 95\% interval comes from the directory-level bootstrap of Appendix~\ref{app:ci_pq}.
Per-cell values are in Table~\ref{tab:pq_routing}.

\paragraph{No threshold.}
The existing-versus-new AUROC of each test witness's best-match cosine rises by 0.037 (0.021 to 0.055) for LaBSE, 0.055 (0.034 to 0.077) for Qwen3-0.6B, and 0.027 (0.012 to 0.043) for KaLM-mini.

\paragraph{Best test threshold.}
At the baseline cells the train-fit threshold comes within 1.0 point of the best test threshold.
When both cells use their best test threshold, ABTT still raises assignment accuracy by 7.0 points (4.2 to 9.3) for LaBSE, 8.9 (6.1 to 11.7) for Qwen3-0.6B, and 4.4 (2.2 to 6.8) for KaLM-mini.

\paragraph{The training mean.}
Centering alone ($D=0$) at the baseline cell's layer changes assignment accuracy by $-4.7$ points ($-7.4$ to $-2.0$) for LaBSE, $+2.6$ ($-0.4$ to $5.5$) for Qwen3-0.6B, and $-1.9$ ($-3.7$ to $-0.1$) for KaLM-mini.
At the ABTT cell's layer, the removed components add 12.1, 25.9 and 12.0 points on top of centering (Table~\ref{tab:headline_ci_diffs}).

\paragraph{Hubness.}
From the baseline cell to the ABTT cell, the skewness of the 10-occurrence distribution \citep{radovanovic2010hubs} falls from 2.0--2.5 to 0.6--1.1, while centering alone at the ABTT layer leaves it near 1.8.
Across representations, low hubness does not track good routing.
The character n-gram reference has a skewness of 10.6 and routes as well as the best projected encoder, and SIF raises skewness in LaTa and PhilTa while it raises their routing.
We therefore report the drop in hubness as an observation and do not test it as a cause.

\section{Reference Systems}
\label{app:reference_systems}

Table~\ref{tab:headline} carries four reference rows below the six encoders: one supervised fine-tuning ceiling for each of the three fine-tuned models, and the character 3--5-gram TF-IDF reference.
This appendix describes them.
Table~\ref{tab:finetune_ceiling} gives the three fine-tuning ceilings with the zero-shot rows they are compared against.
Some routing numbers are means over \emph{five reseedings}: each reseeding keeps the training/test split and redraws, within each directory's test witnesses, which serve as queries and which as references; queries are routed against the reference directories.
Every model we fine-tuned is reported, on whichever side of its own zero-shot row it lands.

Three encoders are fine-tuned under one recipe, LaTa, Qwen3-0.6B and KaLM-mini. Fine-tuning only the Latin-pretrained model would leave the ceiling confounded with Latin pretraining, so the second model is a multilingual decoder-only encoder with no Latin-specific pretraining, Qwen3-0.6B. The third answers the remaining question, whether supervision can clear the best cell the correction produces anywhere in the paper: KaLM-mini holds it, at 91.7 assignment accuracy and at 89.4 DirAcc@1 tied with Qwen3-0.6B. All three are trained with symmetric InfoNCE over positive pairs and in-batch negatives (no two pairs from one directory in a batch), temperature 0.05, AdamW at learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$ with weight decay 0.01, linear warmup over the first 10 percent of steps then linear decay, gradient clipping at 1.0, 16 pairs per batch, up to 8 epochs with early stopping after 3 epochs without dev improvement, and seed 42. All three are mean-pooled with the same token filter as their zero-shot rows, so the fine-tuned vectors live in the space those rows are measured in; Qwen3-0.6B and KaLM-mini add gradient checkpointing, which recomputes activations rather than storing them and leaves the gradients unchanged. The dev slice holds out 28 of the 190 train directories with at least two files (71 files), the same directories for every model, so each trains on 499 of the 565 positive train pairs, and the checkpoint is chosen by dev DirAcc@1 with dev AUROC as the tie-break. Accuracy differences at or below one dev file are treated as ties, because one file out of 71 is the pool's whole resolution; without that, a model whose zero-shot encoder already routes all 71 dev files can never select a trained checkpoint. A 71-file pool is the weak link in this protocol: one borderline file flipped between two otherwise identical Qwen3-0.6B runs at a training loss of $6\times10^{-4}$, which is GPU non-determinism rather than a difference in the model, and it is worth 1.4 points of the selection criterion. LaTa selects epoch 7, the last epoch run before early stopping fired, and its ABTT sweep selects $D=10$, the top of the grid, at every layer, so the tuned and fixed ABTT variants coincide there. Qwen3-0.6B selects epoch 3 of 6 run, and its sweep is a genuine one, selecting the top of the grid at 12 of 28 layers and $D=2$ at the layer its routing row reports. KaLM-mini selects epoch 1 of 4 run, the only model of the three whose zero-shot encoder leaves the dev pool room to improve, and its sweep selects the top of the grid at 19 of 24 layers including the layer its routing row reports. All three rows are ceilings at this training budget rather than asymptotes: training loss reaches $4\times10^{-5}$ on Qwen3-0.6B by epoch 4 and $3\times10^{-5}$ on KaLM-mini, so the 499 pairs are memorized and more epochs buy nothing.

The whole project, exploratory runs included, used about 160 GPU-hours and under 200 CPU core-hours.
Every post-hoc analysis (ABTT, whitening, zeroing, bootstrap intervals) ran on CPU from cached vectors.

\section{SIF and Whitening Baselines}
\label{app:sif_variants}

Section~\ref{sec:testbed} names SIF frequency weighting and reduced-rank whitening as baselines to ABTT, and this appendix gives both.
Tables~\ref{tab:taskA_appendix_sif}, \ref{tab:taskB_routing_appendix_sif} and~\ref{tab:taskB_ranking_appendix_mseed} report SIF alone, SIF followed by ABTT with $D=10$, and SIF followed by ABTT with $D$ tuned per layer, at every layer of the six panel models, with one primary metric per task.

\paragraph{SIF.}
SIF acts before pooling.
It can down-weight the frequent tokens that feed the dominant direction (Appendix~\ref{app:depth_extra}), but it removes no direction from the pooled vector.
At every collapsed layer it stays below ABTT.

\paragraph{Shortlist accuracy.}
In the shortlist form of routing, each directory receives its best witness match score, a pseudo-option \texttt{NEW} receives score $\tau$, and the simulated expert succeeds when the labeled directory appears in the shortlist.
Shortlist accuracy, DirAcc@$K$, is the fraction of test queries whose labeled directory is among the top $K$ options.
Table~\ref{tab:taskb} gives this shortlist view under SIF+ABTT, over five reseedings (Appendix~\ref{app:reference_systems}).
DirAcc@2 ranges from 95.1 to 96.1 percent across the six models, and little is gained after DirAcc@3.

\paragraph{Whitening.}
Full-rank PCA whitening \citep{su2021whitening} is ill-conditioned here, since it estimates 768 to 1,024 dimensions from 847 training vectors (condition numbers $3\times10^{5}$ to $10^{16}$).
It ranks below ABTT at every layer: its test AUROC is 0.820--0.876 for the T5 encoders and LaBSE, and 0.552--0.643 for the two decoders.
It also degenerates the routing threshold.
Full-rank whitening leaves the different-directory cosine mean at or below 0.015, so across all 100 whitening cells every file is routed to an existing directory, existing accuracy is 1.000, and new accuracy never exceeds one file in 323.
Assignment accuracy then sits at the 62.4 percent class prior.
That prior beats the baseline in 60 of 100 cells while DirAcc@1 never exceeds 41.5 percent, so the apparent gain is a thresholding artifact.
Whitening to a reduced dimension, as \citet{su2021whitening} also do, drops the near-null directions and is the fairer comparison (Table~\ref{tab:whiten_reduced}).
Fit on training embeddings with $r=128$ or $256$ components, it ranks on par with ABTT.
At each model's train-selected layer, $r=128$ reaches test AUROC 0.973--0.984, against 0.971--0.987 for ABTT.
Over all 100 panel layers, collapsed layers included, both $r=128$ and $r=256$ stay within 0.021 of ABTT; $r=64$ falls slightly below.
On ranking, the repair therefore does not depend on ABTT in particular.
On routing it does.
At each model's train-selected routing layer, $r=128$ reaches DirAcc@1 1.9 to 4.5 points below ABTT, and $r=256$ up to 15 points below.
We therefore keep ABTT as the reference correction.
