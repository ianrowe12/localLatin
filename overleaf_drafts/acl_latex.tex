\begin{abstract}
Practitioners often read intermediate layers of off-the-shelf encoders, check them for anisotropy with mean pairwise cosine, and correct the embedding space post hoc.
We test this practice on medieval Latin canon law, where a scholarly edition labels which manuscript copies (witnesses) share a source.
In the raw T5 encoders LaTa, PhilTa and mT5-base, 26 of 36 layers rank same-source pairs near chance, although their first and last layers retrieve well.
Mean pairwise cosine gives no reliable warning of this collapse, while the share of variance on the first principal component is high at every collapsed layer.
The signal is masked, not missing: a projection fit on training embeddings alone brings all 100 layers of our six-encoder panel to AUROC 0.962--0.987.
Of the six raw T5-family encoders we test, four collapse: these three and T5-v1.1-base.
All four share the T5 v1.1 layout; encoder-only siblings pretrained on the same corpora and two checkpoints with the original T5 layout do not.
Removing about three principal directions restores every collapsed layer, while zeroing the ten largest coordinates restores none.
After the projection the six encoders route witnesses to their source or to ``new'' about equally well, but no frozen encoder at its train-selected layer beats a character n-gram reference.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Off-the-shelf encoders are a common tool for finding reused text in digital-humanities collections \citep[e.g.,][]{manjavacas2019allusive,schelb2026locisimiles}.
Practitioners often read an intermediate layer, because intermediate layers can outperform the final one \citep{skean2025layer}.
They check the space for anisotropy, usually with the mean cosine between embeddings \citep{ethayarajh2019contextual}.
They then correct it, for example by removing the top principal components \citep{mu2018allbutthetop} or by whitening \citep{su2021whitening}.
This practice assumes that the check warns when a layer retrieves poorly.
Mean cosine is already known to be a poor isotropy measure \citep{rudman2022isoscore}; we ask whether it, or any label-free statistic, warns where retrieval fails.

Medieval Latin suits this test.
The Carolingian Canon Law project \citep[CCL;][]{firey2009ccl} keys each transcribed manuscript unit to the source it copies, with expert labels that no model shaped.
Latin also offers T5 and encoder-only models from the same authors and pretraining corpora.
Our testbed holds 1,705 \emph{witnesses}, manuscript copies of 840 sources.
We evaluate two tasks: \emph{ranking} pairs of witnesses by cosine, scored by AUROC, and \emph{routing} each witness to its source or to ``new''.
Because witnesses copy their source's wording, a character n-gram reference ranks pairs at 0.987 AUROC, so a retrieval failure points to the representation.

Three raw T5 encoders, trained with no embedding objective, fail at mid-depth (Figure~\ref{fig:depth}).
In LaTa, PhilTa and mT5-base \citep{xue2021mt5}, 26 of 36 layers rank same-source pairs at AUROC 0.50 to 0.66, near chance.
Their first and last layers still retrieve.
Three embedding-trained encoders, which complete our six-model panel, never collapse.
The usual check, mean pairwise cosine, gives no reliable warning in either direction.
The share of variance on the first principal component (top-PC share) is high at every collapsed layer: necessary for collapse in our pretrained models, but not sufficient.

The signal is masked, not missing.
All-but-the-Top \citep[ABTT;][]{mu2018allbutthetop}, a linear projection fit on training embeddings only, removes the mean and the top principal components from every embedding, with no parameter update.
It brings all 100 panel layers to AUROC 0.962 to 0.987, the 26 collapsed layers included.
Because the projection sees no test label, the ranking it recovers is already present in each collapsed layer.

Among the six raw T5-family encoders we test, the collapse tracks the T5 v1.1 layout: a gated-GELU feed-forward block \citep{shazeer2020glu} and untied input and output embeddings \citep{t5v11release}.
LaBERTa and PhilBERTa, encoder-only siblings of LaTa and PhilTa, never fall below 0.826 and 0.883.
T5-v1.1-base collapses on the same Latin text (lowest AUROC 0.489), while the original T5-base \citep{raffel2020t5} never falls below 0.816.
T5-base also saw supervised tasks in pretraining, so we add T5-efficient-base \citep{tay2022scale}, with T5-base's layout but pretrained on C4 alone, without dropout and on about 30 times fewer tokens; it does not collapse either.
Of the six raw T5-family encoders we test, the four that collapse all have this layout.
We do not isolate which change matters.

Inside the collapsed layers, about three directions mask the signal.
Removing three principal components brings all 26 layers to AUROC 0.914 or above, and mT5-base keeps improving up to ten.
Zeroing the ten largest coordinates restores none of them, although the first principal component (the dominant direction) sits mostly on such coordinates.
In LaTa, pooling without the comma, the period and \texttt{</s>} restores every collapsed layer; in mT5-base, dropping up to its top 100 token types does not.

This paper makes four contributions.
(1)~A per-layer retrieval profile of T5 encoder states, the first we know of; it shows a mid-depth collapse that mean cosine misses, top-PC share flags and ABTT reverses (Section~\ref{sec:depth}).
(2)~Controls showing that the collapse tracks the T5 v1.1 layout: T5-v1.1-base collapses where the original T5-base does not, encoder-only siblings on the same corpora do not, and the high-share middle layers recur on English text (Section~\ref{sec:models}).
(3)~A localization of the mask to about three directions, which zeroing the ten largest coordinates does not remove, with a token-level account in LaTa and none in mT5-base (Section~\ref{sec:localizing}).
(4)~A measure of what the projection gains for routing and where it stops, set against a character n-gram reference (Section~\ref{sec:routing}).
We will release the frozen testbed, split, evaluation code and character n-gram reference scores.
We localize and repair the collapse but do not explain why it tracks the T5 v1.1 layout.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:testbed}

\paragraph{Corpus and labels.}
\label{sec:dataset}
The testbed comes from CCL \citep{firey2009ccl}, an open-access edition of early medieval, mostly ninth-century, canon-law manuscripts.
A \emph{witness} is one manuscript's copy of a source text, kept as one file.
Witnesses that copy the same source share a \emph{directory}; the testbed holds 1,705 witnesses in 840 directories.
The labels are expert-assigned by the project and checked by collation in its parallel display (Appendix~\ref{app:corpus_provenance}).

\paragraph{Split and train-only rule.}
\label{sec:setup}
\label{sec:layers}
We split the witnesses into 847 training and 858 test witnesses, with 565 and 596 same-source pairs; pairs form only within a split (Appendix~\ref{app:testbed_details}).
The layer, routing threshold and ABTT are chosen or fit on the training split.
The test split selects nothing.

\paragraph{Tasks and metrics.}
\label{sec:tasks}
\emph{Ranking} scores every pair of test witnesses by the cosine of their mean-pooled embeddings.
We report AUROC with same-source pairs as positives.
\emph{Routing} is open-set: each test witness goes to an existing directory or to ``new'', and DirAcc@1 is the share of correct decisions (Section~\ref{sec:routing} gives the procedure).

A \emph{collapsed layer} has \emph{baseline} (uncorrected) test AUROC below 0.70.
The cutoff only describes layers; it selects no reported number.
Two label-free statistics, computed on the training embeddings, describe each layer.
The \emph{mean pairwise cosine} is the usual anisotropy check \citep{ethayarajh2019contextual}.
The \emph{top-PC share} is the share of variance on the first principal component of the centered embeddings.

\input{tables/models_main}

\paragraph{Models.}
Of the eleven models in Table~\ref{tab:models}, six form the \emph{panel}, analyzed in full over 100 layers.
Three are raw T5 encoders with no embedding objective: the Latin LaTa \citep{lata2023card}, its Ancient Greek, Latin and English sibling PhilTa \citep{riemenschneider2023lata}, and mT5-base \citep{xue2021mt5}.
Three are embedding-trained: LaBSE \citep{feng2022labse}, Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B \citep[Qwen3-0.6B;][]{qwen3embedding2025} and KaLM-mini \citep{kalm2025embedding}.
The other five are raw: T5-efficient-base and four controls, each matched to another model in Section~\ref{sec:models}: the encoder-only siblings LaBERTa \citep{laberta2023card} and PhilBERTa \citep{riemenschneider2023lata}, and the English T5 encoders T5-v1.1-base \citep{t5v11release} and the original T5-base \citep{raffel2020t5}.
We mean-pool the hidden states of every transformer block, dropping only padding and tokens that decode to no text, such as SentencePiece's bare word-boundary piece; special tokens such as \texttt{</s>} and punctuation are kept.
Appendix~\ref{app:lasttok} compares last-token pooling.
Table~\ref{tab:all_models} lists all thirteen models, adding Sentence-T5 \citep{ni2022sentencet5} and SPhilBERTa \citep{riemenschneider2023sphilberta}.

\paragraph{Post-processing.}
\label{sec:postproc}
ABTT \citep{mu2018allbutthetop} subtracts the training mean and removes the top $D$ principal components of the centered training embeddings from every vector.
We choose $D \in \{1,2,3,5,7,10\}$ per layer by training DirAcc@1.
ABTT is fit on training embeddings only and updates no parameter; we also call it \emph{the projection}.
We compare SIF frequency weighting \citep{arora2017sif} and reduced-rank whitening \citep{su2021whitening} in Appendix~\ref{app:sif_variants}.

\section{Collapse by Depth}
\label{sec:depth}

In LaTa, PhilTa and mT5-base most middle layers rank near chance.
Mean cosine gives no reliable warning, but top-PC share is high at every collapsed layer.
ABTT restores ranking at every panel layer.

\paragraph{LaTa, PhilTa and mT5-base fall to near chance at mid-depth.}
Figure~\ref{fig:depth} gives baseline ranking AUROC at every layer of the six panel models.
In LaTa, PhilTa and mT5-base, 26 of the 36 layers collapse, ranking at AUROC 0.50 to 0.66.
They are layers 2--11 of LaTa, 3--11 of PhilTa and 5--11 of mT5-base.
The lowest remaining panel layer ranks at 0.799 (mT5-base, layer~4), so any cutoff from 0.66 to 0.79 gives the same 26 layers.
Training AUROC below 0.70 picks the same 26 layers.
The first and last layers still retrieve; LaTa ranks at 0.934 at layer~1 and 0.938 at layer~12.
The embedding-trained panel models never rank below 0.806.

\paragraph{A train-only projection restores ranking at every layer of the six panel models.}
ABTT (Section~\ref{sec:postproc}) lifts all 100 layers of the panel, the 26 collapsed layers included, to AUROC 0.962 to 0.987 (Figure~\ref{fig:depth}, solid lines).
Reduced-rank whitening (128 or 256 components), fit on the same training embeddings, ranks within 0.021 of ABTT at every panel layer (Appendix~\ref{app:sif_variants}).
The narrow band holds for ranking only.
Routing after ABTT still varies by layer, from 75.6 to 90.2 DirAcc@1 (Appendix~\ref{app:per_layer}).

\paragraph{Mean pairwise cosine gives no reliable warning.}
Figure~\ref{fig:diagnostics}a plots training-set mean pairwise cosine against baseline test AUROC.
Collapsed panel layers have a lower mean cosine (0.22 to 0.58) than any embedding-trained panel layer (0.59 to 0.97).
But LaTa's healthy layers~1 and~12 sit in the collapsed range (0.50 and 0.58).
T5-v1.1-base, which also collapses (Table~\ref{tab:models}; Section~\ref{sec:models}), sits at 0.87 to 0.96 at every layer, collapsed or not.
Neither a high nor a low mean cosine marks a collapsed layer.

\paragraph{Top-PC share is high at every collapsed layer of the pretrained models.}
Top-PC share needs no labels either (Figure~\ref{fig:diagnostics}b).
It is at least 0.76 at every collapsed layer of the panel and of T5-v1.1-base.
In the three embedding-trained panel models it peaks at 0.524 (LaBSE).
A high share is necessary for collapse in our pretrained models but not sufficient: of the 88 healthy layers in Figure~\ref{fig:diagnostics}, only mT5-base layer~4 reaches 0.76 (0.893, AUROC 0.799).
Sentence-T5 \citep{ni2022sentencet5}, an embedding-trained T5 encoder, has a share of 0.76 or more at layers 2--9 (peak 0.94) yet ranks at 0.800 or above at every layer (Table~\ref{tab:all_models}).
Among collapsed layers a higher share does not predict a lower AUROC (Appendix~\ref{app:depth_extra}).

\section{Collapse Across Models}
\label{sec:models}

Of the eleven models in Table~\ref{tab:models}, four collapse: LaTa, PhilTa, mT5-base and T5-v1.1-base.
All four have the \emph{T5 v1.1 layout}: a gated-GELU feed-forward \citep{shazeer2020glu} and untied input and output embeddings \citep{t5v11release} (Table~\ref{tab:all_models}).
None of the other seven collapses at any layer.
T5-base and T5-efficient-base keep a ReLU feed-forward and tied embeddings.

\paragraph{Encoder-only siblings pretrained on the same corpora do not collapse.}
LaBERTa and PhilBERTa share authors and pretraining corpora with LaTa and PhilTa \citep{lata2023card,laberta2023card,riemenschneider2023lata} and, like them, have no embedding objective.
LaTa and PhilTa fall to 0.496 and 0.538 AUROC; the siblings never fall below 0.826 (LaBERTa) and 0.883 (PhilBERTa).
Their top-PC share peaks at 0.254 and 0.162 (vs.\ 0.952 and 0.858 in LaTa and PhilTa).
Shared pretraining text and a missing embedding objective do not produce the collapse on their own.
Architecture, layout included, pretraining objective and tokenizer vary together within each pair, so the pairs cannot say which matters (Appendix~\ref{app:models}).

\paragraph{T5-v1.1-base collapses; T5-base and T5-efficient-base, with the original layout, do not.}
T5-v1.1-base and T5-base share their tokenizer, the span-corruption objective and C4 pretraining text \citep{raffel2020t5,t5v11release}.
On Latin text, T5-v1.1-base falls to 0.489 at layer 2, and its layers 2 to 11 collapse.
T5-base stays at 0.816 or above at every layer, with top-PC share peaking at 0.545.
The two differ in layout and in pretraining mix: T5-base also saw supervised tasks \citep{raffel2020t5}.
To separate layout from that mix we add T5-efficient-base \citep{tay2022scale}: T5-base's layout, but pretrained like T5-v1.1-base on C4 alone and without dropout, and on about 30 times fewer tokens than either.
At that budget it does not collapse: its lowest AUROC (0.736) and its peak top-PC share (0.627) both fall at layer 11 (Table~\ref{tab:d2_t5_efficient}).
On the four controls in Table~\ref{tab:models}, ABTT lifts every layer to AUROC 0.927 or above, the ten collapsed layers of T5-v1.1-base included, but eight of the 48 (seven in T5-v1.1-base) stay below the panel's band (0.962--0.987; Table~\ref{tab:d2_controls_abtt}).
Among the six raw T5-family encoders we test, the collapse tracks the T5 v1.1 layout; we do not isolate which change is responsible.

\paragraph{In three models with the T5 v1.1 layout, high top-PC share in the middle layers appears on English text too.}
T5-v1.1-base was pretrained on English alone, so its high-share layers on Latin could reflect a language mismatch.
We read mT5-base, PhilTa and T5-v1.1-base on 847 English passages of US court opinions \citep{cap2024,kandpal2025commonpile}, paired with the Latin training witnesses and matched in mT5 token length.
On English, in all three models, layers 4 to 11 have a top-PC share of at least 0.82 and the first and last layers stay at or below 0.34 (Table~\ref{tab:gen_geometry}, Figure~\ref{fig:gen_geometry}).
At layers 2 to 11 of T5-v1.1-base, top-PC share is 0.931 to 0.950 on English and 0.958 to 0.975 on Latin.
The check covers geometry only; without labels, it says nothing about English retrieval.

\paragraph{In LaTa, contrastive fine-tuning leaves the middle layers collapsed.}
In a small test, we fine-tune LaTa on 499 training pairs, adding the contrastive embedding objective it lacks (Appendix~\ref{app:reference_systems}).
Fine-tuning lifts the last layer from 0.938 to 0.984 AUROC but leaves layers 2 to 11 at 0.50 to 0.57.
Peak top-PC share barely moves (0.952 before, 0.945 after; Table~\ref{tab:ft_lata_layerwise}).
\citet{haemmerl2023anisotropy} report the same split: their sentence-tuned multilingual encoder keeps a large outlier dimension at a middle layer but none at its output layer.

\section{Localizing the Collapse}
\label{sec:localizing}

In the collapsed layers about three directions mask the retrieval signal.
Zeroing the ten largest coordinates does not remove this mask, and a few token types feed it in LaTa but not in mT5-base.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_localize}
\caption{Test ranking AUROC at the 26 collapsed layers of LaTa (10 layers), PhilTa (9) and mT5-base (7). Lines and bands: median and range per model; dashed line: AUROC 0.90; gray line: chance. (a)~Zeroing the $k$ coordinates of largest mean absolute value: no layer passes 0.90 by $k=10$ (vertical line). (b)~Removing the top $D$ principal components (ticks evenly spaced, $D$ values not): from $D=3$ every layer passes 0.90.}
\label{fig:localize}
\end{figure*}

We run each test at the 26 collapsed layers, fit every intervention on training embeddings, and read test ranking AUROC.
A layer counts as restored at AUROC 0.90 or above.
Appendix~\ref{app:localizing} gives further tables and controls.

\paragraph{Zeroing the ten largest coordinates restores no collapsed layer.}
In pretrained transformers a few outlier coordinates dominate cosine similarity \citep{timkey2021rogue}.
Zeroing them improves pooled similarity in pretrained encoders \citep{luo2021positional}, and whitening does better still \citep{haemmerl2023anisotropy}.
In our encoders, zeroing up to ten coordinates, ranked on training embeddings by variance or by mean absolute value, restores none of the 26, and the best layer reaches 0.794 (Figure~\ref{fig:localize}a).
The dominant direction loads mostly on these coordinates: the ten of highest variance carry a median 92\% of its squared loading.
Yet after zeroing them, its remainder still holds a median 72\% of the remaining variance.
Zeroing far more coordinates does restore LaTa and PhilTa.
Ranked by variance, recovery starts at 50 coordinates, and their 19 collapsed layers all recover by 300 (by 400 under the mean-absolute-value ranking of Figure~\ref{fig:localize}a).
No mT5-base layer recovers even at 400.
The dominant direction loads mostly on a few coordinates but acts beyond them, which extends the outlier-dimension account.

\paragraph{Removing three directions restores every collapsed layer.}
ABTT removes whole directions instead of coordinates, so we vary the number $D$ of principal components it removes (Figure~\ref{fig:localize}b).
Centering alone ($D=0$) does not help: the median AUROC over the collapsed layers falls from 0.541 to 0.503.
At these layers the first component holds a median 94\% of the centered training variance.
It is not mainly a passage-length direction: $|\rho| \le 0.44$ with log token count at all 26 layers.
Removing it alone recovers a median 45\% of the AUROC gain from removing ten components, and 80\% or more at only one of the 26 layers.
Removing three components brings all 26 layers to 0.914 or above.
mT5-base keeps improving up to ten components (median 0.934 at three, 0.978 at ten).
The ten removed components rank at 0.489, the next ten at 0.919, and the retained subspace at 0.977 (Table~\ref{tab:e3_subspace_split}).
At these layers the signal lies outside the removed directions.

\paragraph{In LaTa, three token types feed the removed directions.}
A pooled vector is the mean of its token vectors, so a few token types with large values along the removed directions could dominate a witness's scores on those directions.
We rank token types by their share of the variance of training-witness scores on the top three components, then pool without the top $m$ types, for $m$ up to 100.
We ask whether some $m$ restores the collapsed layers.
In LaTa, $m=3$ does.
Pooling without the comma, the period and \texttt{</s>} drops 11\% of the test tokens and restores all 10 collapsed layers (median 0.919).
Random token types of the same training share leave the median at 0.500.
The tokens are carriers, not a cause, since LaBERTa also pools commas, periods and \texttt{</s>} and does not collapse (Section~\ref{sec:models}).
PhilTa needs more types: dropping its top 30, about a third of its tokens, restores all 9 of its collapsed layers on test passages (settings chosen per layer on training AUROC restore every collapsed layer of both models, medians 0.926 and 0.928).

\paragraph{In mT5-base, dropping up to its top 100 token types restores no collapsed layer.}
Dropping up to 100 token types, ranked the same way, restores none of its 7 collapsed layers, the best reaching 0.748.
mT5-base also resists coordinate zeroing, yet removing three to ten directions repairs it.
Its mask sits neither in a few coordinates nor in a few token types, and we have no token-level account.

\section{Routing and Limits}
\label{sec:routing}

At their train-selected layers after ABTT, the six frozen encoders route about equally well, but none beats a character n-gram reference.

Of the 858 test witnesses, 535 have a same-source partner in test (\emph{existing}) and 323 do not (\emph{new}).
Each test directory scores a witness by the highest cosine between the witness and any other member.
A pseudo-directory \texttt{NEW} scores a fixed threshold $\tau$, chosen to maximize training F1 at separating same-source from different-source pairs.
The witness goes to the highest-scoring directory.
DirAcc@1 counts a decision as correct if it names the labeled directory, or \texttt{NEW} for a new witness.
Assignment accuracy scores only the existing-versus-new decision; always answering existing scores 62.4\%.
Each headline number reads a model at its \emph{train-selected} layer: the one of highest training AUROC for ranking, or of highest training DirAcc@1 for routing (Table~\ref{tab:selected_layers}).
The task is lexically solvable by design, since the witnesses of one source copy its wording.
Our \emph{character n-gram reference}, TF-IDF over character 3--5-grams fit on training witnesses, reaches 0.987 AUROC and 89.9 DirAcc@1 (Table~\ref{tab:lexical_baselines}).

\input{tables/headline_main}

\paragraph{At train-selected layers, routing after the projection converges across the six encoders.}
At the train-selected layers of Table~\ref{tab:headline}, the DirAcc@1 spread across the six encoders falls from 39.3 points without correction (95\% CI 34.6 to 43.7) to 3.3 after ABTT (1.8 to 6.0).
Uncorrected, they run from 46.6 (mT5-base) to 85.9 (KaLM-mini); after ABTT, from 86.1 to 89.4.
Every encoder's routing gain has a bootstrap interval excluding zero, from 3.5 points for KaLM-mini (1.0 to 6.0) to 41.8 for mT5-base (Table~\ref{tab:headline_ci_diffs}).

\needspace{3\baselineskip}
\paragraph{For the embedding-trained encoders, the routing gain does not depend on the threshold.}
LaBSE, Qwen3-0.6B and KaLM-mini already rank well without correction (AUROC 0.956 to 0.972), so their routing gain cannot come from a ranking repair.
ABTT still raises their DirAcc@1 by 7.0, 9.1 and 3.5 points.
Because the gain could come from a better-placed threshold $\tau$, we also score the existing-versus-new decision without one, as the AUROC of each test witness's best-match cosine, existing against new.
ABTT raises it by 0.037 for LaBSE, 0.055 for Qwen3-0.6B and 0.027 for KaLM-mini, and every paired interval excludes zero (Appendix~\ref{app:routing_no_threshold}; per-cell values in Table~\ref{tab:pq_routing}).
ABTT's gain in assignment accuracy also holds at each setting's best test threshold and under a finer threshold grid (Appendix~\ref{app:ci_pq}).
For these three encoders, centering alone ($D=0$) scores 12 to 26 points lower in assignment accuracy than ABTT at the same layer (Table~\ref{tab:headline_ci_diffs}).
We do not test what in the removed components produces this gain.

\paragraph{No frozen encoder at its train-selected layer beats a character n-gram reference.}
The best ABTT results in Table~\ref{tab:headline} tie the reference: LaBSE ranks at 0.987, and Qwen3-0.6B and KaLM-mini route at 89.4, with every difference interval including zero.
Two ranking cells fall below it with intervals excluding zero, LaTa and Qwen3-0.6B (by 0.016 and 0.014 AUROC); mT5-base falls 0.012 below, its interval touching zero (0.000 to 0.027).
LaTa's routing cell falls 3.7 points below it (Table~\ref{tab:headline_ci_diffs}).
Our claims thus concern where the signal sits in the encoders and what masks it.

\paragraph{Fine-tuning Qwen3-0.6B and KaLM-mini adds about two routing points over the projection.}
As a supervised reference, we fine-tune LaTa, Qwen3-0.6B and KaLM-mini contrastively on 499 training pairs (Appendix~\ref{app:reference_systems}).
For routing, fine-tuning plus ABTT beats zero-shot ABTT by 1.8 points for Qwen3-0.6B (92.3 against 90.5) and by 2.1 for KaLM-mini (93.0 against 90.9); on LaTa the two tie at 87.7.
These are means over five reseedings, each a new random draw of which test witnesses are queries and which are the references they are routed against (Table~\ref{tab:finetune_ceiling}).
In the single-split cells of Table~\ref{tab:headline} the gains are 2.0 and 3.1 points (only KaLM-mini's interval excludes zero), and LaTa falls 0.9.
These references are flattered: 206 of the 535 existing test witnesses (38.5\%) sit in a directory that supplied a training pair.
Fine-tuned Qwen3-0.6B and KaLM-mini rank above the n-gram reference in point estimate (0.996 and 0.997 AUROC).
After fine-tuning, ABTT no longer helps ranking: AUROC falls slightly in point estimate in all three models (by 0.002 to 0.014; every interval includes zero), in line with \citet{rajaee2021finetuning}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Before reading an intermediate layer of a frozen encoder, fit a projection on training embeddings.
In the six panel models it scored above the baseline in point estimate at all 100 layers in ranking and routing (Appendix~\ref{app:per_layer}), which makes it cheap insurance.
Routing after it still varies by layer, so the layer still matters.
To screen uncorrected states, check top-PC share, not mean pairwise cosine.
In our models mean cosine gave no reliable warning in either direction, while every collapsed layer of the pretrained models had a top-PC share of at least 0.76 (Section~\ref{sec:depth}).
The 0.76 is the lowest share we observed at a collapsed layer of a pretrained checkpoint, not a cutoff validated on held-out models.
A high share is necessary for collapse here but not sufficient, and does not order layers inside the collapsed regime.
Choosing among layers still needs a train-only task signal, such as training AUROC.

Why models with the T5 v1.1 layout collapse remains open.
Gated feed-forward blocks are reported to amplify outlier activations in decoder language models \citep{fishman2025fp8,yang2025activationspikes}, but the original T5 also carries large outliers \citep{zhao2025outlier}.
Our localization names carriers in LaTa but no cause.
Concurrently, \citet{mikkelsen2026clinical} reports a mid-depth retrieval trough on clinical text; retrieval-trained encoders recover in the last layers, and encoders without a retrieval objective recover only partly.
In our models, by contrast, neither the embedding-trained encoders nor the encoder-only siblings collapse, and it tests no T5 model.

For the editor of a text collection, ABTT makes the choice among these six encoders matter little (Section~\ref{sec:routing}; maps in Appendix~\ref{app:cluster_geometry}).
The existing-versus-new decision is still wrong for about one test witness in ten (assignment accuracy 88.5 to 91.7; Table~\ref{tab:headline_ci}).
The final call stays with the scholar.
Because the task is lexically solvable, a practical tool can start from character n-grams; dense encoders are reported to help where reuse is paraphrastic \citep{delaselle2026transcription}, a case we do not test.

\section*{Limitations}

Four factors are uncontrolled.
(i)~We use one labeled corpus in one language and genre, early medieval Latin canon law; its 1,705 witnesses are many for this field but few by current NLP standards.
(ii)~Within each sibling pair, architecture, pretraining objective and tokenizer vary together, so the pairs do not say which of them matters.
That LaTa and LaBERTa share authors and pretraining corpora rests on their model cards \citep{lata2023card,laberta2023card}.
(iii)~The T5 v1.1 changes (gated-GELU feed-forward with a narrower inner width, untied embeddings) vary together, so we do not say which of them matters.
T5-efficient-base has the original layout without T5-base's supervised pretraining, but it is a single checkpoint pretrained on about 30 times fewer tokens; it separates layout from pretraining mix only at that budget.
(iv)~The token ablation accounts for the masking directions in LaTa with three token types and in PhilTa only with 30, about a third of its tokens; in mT5-base dropping up to its top 100 token types restores no collapsed layer, so its mask has no token-level account.

The scope of three results is narrow.
We claim the narrow band for the six-model panel only: on the four controls and T5-efficient-base ABTT lifts every layer to AUROC 0.927 or above, but nine of their 60 layers, seven of them in T5-v1.1-base, stay below the panel's band (0.962 to 0.987).
The English check measures geometry only; we have no labels for English retrieval.
The task is lexically solvable by design, and a character n-gram reference ties the best encoders after ABTT, so we do not claim that encoders beat surface overlap on this corpus.

The fine-tuning test is small (499 pairs, at most eight epochs), and its references are flattered by directory overlap with the training pairs (Section~\ref{sec:routing}).
Our intervals resample test directories with the layer, threshold and ABTT fit held fixed; they cover test sampling and not the choice of split.
ABTT needs enough text to estimate principal components, so smaller archives need separate study.

The labels are expert-assigned, but by one project.
Its director or supervised graduate students assigned the keys, and verification is by collation in the project's parallel display, not by independent double annotation.
No inter-annotator agreement figure therefore exists for this testbed.
Collation needs at least two witnesses of a key, and 545 of the 840 directories hold only one in this testbed.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}

The testbed holds early medieval legal texts, transcribed from manuscripts and early printings, and contains no personal data.
The Carolingian Canon Law project publishes its material under a Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial-ShareAlike 4.0 license \citep{ccl2026about}.
The project has confirmed in writing that the license covers the transcriptions, annotations and markup in the exports we use and that, in the view of the project's Advisory Board, attribution to the project suffices; the project's interface credits the transcriber and proofreader of each unit.
The project's corpus keeps growing and being corrected, so the testbed is a dated snapshot (version 1), fixed by a sha256 manifest of its 1,705 labeled files, and every number in this paper comes from it.
We will release it under the same license, in coordination with the project's periodic versioned exports, and later versions will follow those exports.
The intended use is research on retrieval and on assisted source identification: the system ranks candidates for an expert to judge and does not identify sources on its own.
The English control sample consists of public-domain US court opinions from the Caselaw Access Project \citep{cap2024}, which name litigants; we use it only for label-free geometry statistics and do not release it.
We used AI assistants for writing and checking code; the authors verified all code and numbers.

\section{Corpus Provenance and Derivation}
\label{app:corpus_provenance}

This appendix records where the testbed text comes from and how our files were derived from it, in the detail that Section~\ref{sec:dataset} leaves out.

\paragraph{The project.} CCL \citep{firey2009ccl} is an open-access digital edition founded in 2009; \citet{eichbauer2014ccl} reviews it.\footnote{Founding year, pre-2019 workflow, proofreading standard, the one manuscript transcribed by handwritten text recognition (HTR), the design of the identifiers, the assignment and verification of keys, and the extent of unidentified material: unpublished documentation and correspondence supplied by the project, 2026.} Its texts are early medieval canon-law manuscripts, most of them ninth-century and never edited to modern standards. Each manuscript is identified by its siglum (Place-Library-Shelfmark), and a witness is one manuscript's copy of a source text, transcribed as one unit (one canon or excerpt).

\paragraph{Transcription.} Transcription is manual, from manuscript images or, for some texts, eighteenth-century printings, with one exception: HTR has been attempted on one manuscript (Paris, BnF, lat.\ 1454; 142 of the 1,705 witnesses), and its transcription is still in proofreading. Before 2019 transcribers worked from purchased microfilm into word-processed files, which CCL contributors converted to TEI-P5 (the Text Encoding Initiative's XML standard for scholarly editions). Since 2019 they transcribe in the CCL Transcription Desk beside a IIIF image of the folio (a zoomable web-served scan of the manuscript leaf); an approved proofreader on the Proofreading Desk then checks the text against the image to the letter and adds TEI markup \citep{ccl2026desks}. That proofreading stage belongs to the post-2019 workflow. The interface credits the transcriber and proofreader of each unit \citep{ccl2026about}.

\paragraph{Identifiers.} The project has two identifier systems, both designed at its launch in 2009 by its director in consultation with a mathematician, a software developer and a second scholar of early medieval canon law. The first is a location identifier. Every manuscript is a unique object, so every unit has exactly one place in one manuscript, and the identifier records it as the manuscript siglum, the folium, and the unit's position on that folium. Our file names are these identifiers. The second is the source key. Many units in different manuscripts copy the same prescription issued by the same authority, and the key names that common source. It encodes the type of source (a church council, a papal decretal, a royal capitulary, a theologian, a penitential), the point of origin (a particular council, pope or author), the date of origin, and the item's reference in modern scholarship, such as a canon, letter or register number. The key is designed so that a researcher can find the modern authoritative edition of the source where one exists.

\paragraph{Labels.} CCL keys a unit to its original source, such as a fourth-century council or a papal decretal, once that source is identified, and our directories reuse that key as supplied. In a label, a four-letter code carries the type of source and its point of origin, followed by the year and the canon or chapter number, as in \texttt{CTOU.567.16}; some labels carry a trailing annotation, as in \texttt{CTOU.567.16 (15)} or \texttt{CARL.501?.18}. A label counts as key-named when it begins with a four-letter code, a period, a year and a period, whatever follows; by this rule 689 of the 840 directory labels are source keys. The other 151 are modern edition citations, the Apostolic Canons (\texttt{Can.apost.}$N$), references to other collections such as the Capitula Martini, biblical references, and a handful of unidentified sources; each unidentified source is a singleton directory and contributes no positive pairs.

\paragraph{Assignment and verification.} Keys were assigned by the project's director, a specialist in early medieval canon law, or by graduate students working under the director's supervision, who had enough Latin to check a manuscript version against the modern authoritative version of the source. Verification, and occasional correction, runs through the project's parallel display, which sets side by side all units that share a key and shows how far their wording agrees. A misidentified unit stands out there as a text that cannot be a version of the others. This is verification by collation (see Limitations). The project supplied two label corrections during this study, and both are logged with the frozen testbed. We assigned no label ourselves and changed only those two. What remains unidentified is large: across the several hundred manuscripts that contain the project's texts, thousands of units still carry no key, and they are the queries that routing is meant to serve.

\paragraph{Derivation.} We hold the witnesses as plain text derived from CCL TEI-P5 exports. The rule is one file per unit: the directory is the source key from the unit's \texttt{scholSource} division, which is stripped from the text so that no file carries its own label; the text is the manuscript rubric followed by the canon text, with inline markup flattened (editorial notes kept, deletions dropped) and layout whitespace collapsed; a unit without a source key receives no directory. The rule is scripted. On a current export of one manuscript (Paris, BnF, lat.\ 2123), it reproduces all 279 units present in both the export and the corpus character for character after whitespace normalization, and places every one in the same directory.

\section{Experimental Setup Details}
\label{app:testbed_details}

This appendix gives the parts of the setup that Sections~\ref{sec:testbed} and~\ref{sec:routing} leave out: how the split treats directories of each size, how KaLM-mini attends, the further layer statistics, the SIF weighting, and how the lexical reference fares by degree of overlap.

\paragraph{Split.}
Singleton directories (545 of 840) are divided file by file between the two splits.
Doubletons go whole to one side.
Larger directories are divided internally, so that every size class yields training positives.
Pairs form only within a split.

\paragraph{KaLM-mini attention.}
KaLM-mini, a decoder, attends bidirectionally, as its checkpoint's own modeling code specifies.

\paragraph{Further layer statistics.}
Let $p_i = \lambda_i / \sum_j \lambda_j$ for the covariance eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \dots$ of the centered training embeddings; the top-PC share is $p_1$.
The \emph{effective rank} is $\exp(-\sum_i p_i \log p_i)$, the entropy effective rank of \citet{roy2007effectiverank} applied to the covariance eigenvalues.
It is 1 when one direction holds all the variance.
The \emph{cosine gap} $g$ is the mean same-directory test cosine minus the mean different-directory test cosine.
The \emph{standardized gap} $g/s$ divides it by the standard deviation $s$ of all pairwise test cosines, which puts models with different cosine spreads on one scale.
The two gaps need labels; the effective rank, like the top-PC share and the mean pairwise cosine, does not.
Our mean pairwise cosine averages over all pairs of pooled passages, an adaptation of the word-level statistic of \citet{ethayarajh2019contextual}, which uses random pairs.

\paragraph{SIF.}
SIF \citep{arora2017sif} reweights each token by $a/(a+p(w))$ before pooling, with $a=10^{-3}$ and $p(w)$ estimated on training texts.
It gives special tokens, such as the end-of-sequence token \texttt{</s>}, zero weight.
We use only this weighting step of SIF, not its common-component removal.
SIF+ABTT applies both; ABTT-only marks ABTT alone where we contrast the two.

\paragraph{Software.}
We extract hidden states with PyTorch \citep{paszke2019pytorch} and Hugging Face Transformers \citep{wolf2020transformers}, fit whitening and the character TF-IDF reference with scikit-learn \citep{pedregosa2011scikit}, and compute principal components and the remaining statistics with NumPy \citep{harris2020numpy}.
BM25 is our own implementation.

\paragraph{Lexical reference by overlap.}
We cut the positive test pairs into terciles of n-gram overlap, using the TF-IDF score itself.
In every tercile, the character n-gram reference ranks a witness's partner first at least as often as every baseline and ABTT encoder configuration at its train-selected layer.
Table~\ref{tab:lexical_baselines} gives all three lexical scorers.

\paragraph{English text.}

\section{Bootstrap Intervals and Routing Checks}

\paragraph{Intervals.} A bootstrap replicate draws the 514 test directories with replacement ($B=10{,}000$, seed 233). A drawn directory keeps every file and pair: a directory drawn $c$ times weights its files and within-directory pairs by $c$ and its pairs with a directory drawn $c'$ times by $cc'$, and no pair is formed between two copies of one directory. The layer, threshold, ABTT components, $D$, and SIF weights stay fit on the training split, and every score is computed once on the full test set, so a replicate only reweights test pairs and files. Intervals are 95\% percentiles; a paired difference recomputes both cells on each replicate. Before resampling, every cell was recomputed with the paper's evaluation code and matched its printed value. Table~\ref{tab:headline_ci} gives every cell and Table~\ref{tab:headline_ci_diffs} the contrasts quoted in Section~\ref{sec:routing}.

\paragraph{Threshold grid.} We keep the grid of 200 evenly spaced thresholds in $[0,1]$ used for every printed number. Every finer grid optimizes the same training objective, the best F1 over same- against different-directory training pairs, and differs only in resolution. With the layer and $D$ fixed, a grid of 10,001 values moves 45 of the 66 printed routing numbers, by a median of 3 files (0.35 points); ABTT, SIF+ABTT, and fine-tuned cells move by at most 0.8 points, and Base and SIF cells by up to 2.6 (mT5-base's baseline). Re-selecting the layer and $D$ under the new grid as well moves 42 of the 60 encoder routing numbers with the fine grid and 44 with the exact cut; the lexical references have no layer or $D$ to re-select. Refitting would trade one set of differences of one to a few files for another; it changes no conclusion of Section~\ref{sec:routing}.

\section{Reference Systems}
\label{app:reference_systems}

Table~\ref{tab:headline} carries four reference rows below the six encoders: one supervised fine-tuning ceiling for each of the three fine-tuned models, and the character 3--5-gram TF-IDF reference.
This appendix describes them.
Table~\ref{tab:finetune_ceiling} gives the three fine-tuning ceilings with the zero-shot rows they are compared against.
Some routing numbers are means over \emph{five reseedings}: each reseeding keeps the training/test split and redraws, within each directory's test witnesses, which serve as queries and which as references; queries are routed against the reference directories.
Every model we fine-tuned is reported, on whichever side of its own zero-shot row it lands.

Three encoders are fine-tuned under one recipe, LaTa, Qwen3-0.6B and KaLM-mini. Fine-tuning only the Latin-pretrained model would leave the ceiling confounded with Latin pretraining, so the second model is a multilingual decoder-only encoder with no Latin-specific pretraining, Qwen3-0.6B. The third, KaLM-mini, has the best ABTT cell in the paper (91.7 assignment accuracy; 89.4 DirAcc@1, tied with Qwen3-0.6B), so it tests whether supervision can clear that cell.

All three are trained with symmetric InfoNCE over positive pairs and in-batch negatives (no two pairs from one directory in a batch), temperature 0.05, AdamW at learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$ with weight decay 0.01, linear warmup over the first 10 percent of steps then linear decay, gradient clipping at 1.0, 16 pairs per batch, up to 8 epochs with early stopping after 3 epochs without dev improvement, and seed 42. All three are mean-pooled with the same token filter as their zero-shot rows, so the fine-tuned vectors live in the space those rows are measured in; Qwen3-0.6B and KaLM-mini add gradient checkpointing, which recomputes activations rather than storing them and leaves the gradients unchanged.

The dev slice holds out 28 of the 190 train directories with at least two files (71 files), the same directories for every model, so each trains on 499 of the 565 positive train pairs, and the checkpoint is chosen by dev DirAcc@1 with dev AUROC as the tie-break. Accuracy differences at or below one dev file are treated as ties, because one file out of 71 is the pool's whole resolution; without that, a model whose zero-shot encoder already routes all 71 dev files can never select a trained checkpoint. A 71-file pool is the weak link in this protocol: one borderline file flipped between two otherwise identical Qwen3-0.6B runs at a training loss of $6\times10^{-4}$, which is GPU non-determinism rather than a difference in the model, and it is worth 1.4 points of the selection criterion. LaTa selects epoch 7, the last epoch run before early stopping fired, and its ABTT sweep selects $D=10$, the top of the grid, at every layer, so the tuned and fixed ABTT variants coincide there. Qwen3-0.6B selects epoch 3 of 6 run, and its sweep is a genuine one, selecting the top of the grid at 12 of 28 layers and $D=2$ at the layer its routing row reports. KaLM-mini selects epoch 1 of 4 run, the only model of the three whose zero-shot encoder leaves the dev pool room to improve, and its sweep selects the top of the grid at 19 of 24 layers including the layer its routing row reports. All three rows are ceilings at this training budget rather than asymptotes: training loss reaches $4\times10^{-5}$ on Qwen3-0.6B by epoch 4 and $3\times10^{-5}$ on KaLM-mini, so the 499 pairs are memorized and more epochs buy nothing.

\paragraph{Compute.}
Most hidden-state extraction and all fine-tuning ran on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU; the control-model extraction and the token audit ran on a single NVIDIA A40, and T5-efficient-base, with a re-extraction of the four controls for Table~\ref{tab:d2_controls_abtt}, on CPU.
The whole project, exploratory runs included, used about 160 GPU-hours and under 200 CPU core-hours.
Every post-hoc analysis (ABTT, whitening, zeroing, bootstrap intervals) ran on CPU from cached vectors.

\section{SIF and Whitening Baselines}
\label{app:sif_variants}

Section~\ref{sec:testbed} compares SIF frequency weighting and reduced-rank whitening with ABTT; this appendix gives both.
Tables~\ref{tab:taskA_appendix_sif} and~\ref{tab:taskB_routing_appendix_sif} report SIF alone, SIF followed by ABTT with $D=10$, and SIF followed by ABTT with $D$ tuned per layer, at every layer of the six panel models, with one primary metric per task.

Five-reseeding values for ABTT alone, at the train-selected layers of LaTa, Qwen3-0.6B and KaLM-mini, are in Table~\ref{tab:finetune_ceiling}.

\paragraph{SIF.}
SIF acts before pooling.
It can down-weight the frequent tokens that feed the dominant direction (Appendix~\ref{app:depth_extra}), but it removes no direction from the pooled vector.
At every collapsed layer it stays below ABTT.

\paragraph{Shortlist accuracy.}
In the shortlist form of routing, each directory receives its best witness match score, a pseudo-option \texttt{NEW} receives score $\tau$, and the simulated expert succeeds when the labeled directory appears in the shortlist.
Shortlist accuracy, DirAcc@$K$, is the fraction of test queries whose labeled directory is among the top $K$ options.
Table~\ref{tab:taskb} gives this shortlist view under SIF+ABTT, over five reseedings (Appendix~\ref{app:reference_systems}).
DirAcc@2 ranges from 95.1 to 96.1 percent across the six models, and little is gained after DirAcc@3.

\paragraph{Whitening.}
Full-rank PCA whitening \citep{su2021whitening} is ill-conditioned here, since it estimates 768 to 1,024 dimensions from 847 training vectors (condition numbers $3\times10^{5}$ to $10^{16}$).
It ranks below ABTT at every layer: its test AUROC is 0.820--0.876 for the T5 encoders and LaBSE, and 0.552--0.643 for the two decoders.
It also breaks the routing threshold.
Full-rank whitening leaves the different-directory cosine mean at or below 0.015, so across all 100 whitening cells every file is routed to an existing directory, existing accuracy is 1.000, and new accuracy never exceeds one file in 323.
Assignment accuracy then sits at the 62.4 percent class prior.
That prior beats the baseline in 60 of 100 cells while DirAcc@1 never exceeds 41.5 percent, so the apparent gain is a thresholding artifact.
Whitening to a reduced dimension, as \citet{su2021whitening} also do, drops the near-null directions and is the fairer comparison (Table~\ref{tab:whiten_reduced}).
Fit on training embeddings with $r=128$ or $256$ components, it ranks on par with ABTT.
At each model's train-selected layer, $r=128$ reaches test AUROC 0.973--0.984, against 0.971--0.987 for ABTT.
Over all 100 panel layers, collapsed layers included, both $r=128$ and $r=256$ stay within 0.021 of ABTT; $r=64$ falls slightly below.
On ranking, the repair therefore does not depend on ABTT in particular.
On routing it does.
At each model's train-selected routing layer, $r=128$ reaches DirAcc@1 1.9 to 4.5 points below ABTT, and $r=256$ up to 15 points below.
We therefore keep ABTT as the reference correction.

\section{Cluster Geometry}
\label{app:cluster_geometry}

This appendix shows the effect of ABTT on the whole corpus at the routing layers of Table~\ref{tab:headline}.
Figures~\ref{fig:tsne} and~\ref{fig:umap} give the t-SNE \citep{vandermaaten2008tsne} and UMAP \citep{mcinnes2018umap} panels for LaTa, PhilTa, and mT5-base, and Figures~\ref{fig:tsne_appendix} and~\ref{fig:umap_appendix} extend them to LaBSE, Qwen3-0.6B, and KaLM-mini.
All four draw all 1,705 witnesses, color the same six largest directories, and compare baseline mean pooling with ABTT-only at $D=10$.
Each model sits at the layer chosen on the training split by DirAcc@1 under ABTT, the routing ABTT layer of Table~\ref{tab:selected_layers}.
In both maps, ABTT draws the witnesses of each colored directory into tighter groups than baseline mean pooling does.
The maps are two-dimensional, so we read them as illustrations and compute no statistic on them.
