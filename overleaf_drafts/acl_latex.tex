\begin{abstract}



Medieval canon law texts preserve some of the earliest sustained records of judicial reasoning in Western Europe, but they survive in manuscript families shaped by spelling variation, fragmentation, and editorial change. 
As a result, strict semantic matching is a prerequisite for scholarly workflows such as consolidating duplicate passages and linking fragments across witnesses. We introduce a new Medieval Latin dataset of 1,705 canon law fragments grouped into 840 families of meaning-equivalent variants, and evaluate two tasks: duplicate detection and open-set semantic deduplication.
We conduct a layerwise analysis of internal representations across a diverse set of Latin-adapted and multilingual models and uncover a striking failure mode. 
In Latin-adapted T5 encoders, standard mean-pooled hidden states undergo a pronounced mid-layer collapse, causing cosine similarity to lose discriminative power. In contrast, multilingual encoders such as LaBSE and decoder-based models remain substantially more stable.
Motivated by this diagnosis, we apply a simple train-free geometric repair based on All-but-the-Top post-processing, which largely removes the mid-layer failure mode and restores semantic separation.
Finally, we show that the resulting representations support more reliable instance-level attribution using integrated gradients, enabling an inspectable, scholar-facing semantic matching pipeline. 
%Together, our results show that the main bottleneck in Medieval Latin semantic matching is not missing semantic content, but how internal representations are extracted and normalized.



% Semantic matching with sentence embeddings works well in many high-resource settings. Historical text is less forgiving, and layer choice can change the outcome a great deal. We evaluate six pre-trained models from three architecture families on a new Medieval Latin duplicate-detection benchmark with 1,705 canon-law fragments. The set includes two Latin-adapted T5 encoders, a multilingual BERT sentence encoder, a multilingual T5, and two decoder-only models. Mean-pooled embeddings from the Latin T5 encoders collapse sharply in the middle layers, whereas the other models stay much steadier across depth. When we fit All-but-the-Top (ABTT) \citep{mu2018allbutthetop} on the training split and apply it after pooling, most of that collapse disappears and pairwise separation returns. We also revisit directory assignment, where the system offers a top-$k$ shortlist to a human expert, and report how often the labeled directory would be chosen as the model's first, second, third, fourth, fifth, or some other option. To make those rankings easier to inspect, we add example-level integrated-gradient visualizations over original tokens and companion attention maps. Taken together, the results point to representation geometry, not missing semantic content, as the main source of the mid-layer failure.
\end{abstract}

Medieval canon law offers a rich record of early judicial reasoning, reflecting evolving ideas about authority, justice, and social order. Yet, these texts do not exist as stable, single versions. Instead, they are preserved through manuscript traditions that introduce variation in wording, fragmentary transmission, and editorial intervention. 
As a result, passages that express the same legal principle may differ substantially on the surface, while others diverge in subtle but meaningful ways. 
For scholars, identifying strict semantic equivalence across such variants is a fundamental step in consolidating textual evidence, linking fragments, and tracing the transmission of legal thought.


We study this problem by introducing new data of Medieval Latin canon law passages organized into 840 families of meaning-equivalent variants. 
We consider two complementary tasks. \emph{(i)}~Duplicate detection evaluates whether a pair of passages conveys the same meaning under a strict notion of equivalence. 
\emph{(ii)}~Open-set semantic deduplication reflects corpus-level workflows, requiring models to determine whether a newly observed passage matches any existing passage or represents a previously unseen instance. 
%Together, these tasks capture both controlled evaluation and practical usage scenarios in manuscript-driven corpora.


A standard approach is to encode passages using pretrained models and compare them with cosine similarity. While this paradigm is effective for high-resource languages, its behavior in historical languages such as Medieval Latin remains poorly understood. 
In particular, it remains unclear how semantic information is distributed across model depth and whether commonly used representation extraction methods preserve or distort that information. This motivates a closer examination of model internals rather than treating sentence embeddings as fixed outputs.


To this end, we analyze representations across layers for a range of Latin-adapted and multilingual models. 
This analysis reveals a consistent failure mode in Latin-adapted T5 encoders: representations obtained via mean pooling lose discriminative structure in intermediate layers, leading to a collapse in semantic separability. 
Notably, this behavior is not observed to the same extent in multilingual encoders or decoder-based models, which exhibit more stable trends across depth. %These results suggest that performance differences cannot be attributed solely to model pretraining or data, but are strongly influenced by how representations are extracted.



This observation points to a geometric explanation. 
When representations are dominated by a small number of global directions, cosine similarity becomes less sensitive to semantic distinctions, particularly in intermediate layers. 
We address this issue using a simple, train-free correction based on All-but-the-Top (ABTT) post-processing, which removes dominant components from the embedding space. 
This correction restores semantic separation and stabilizes performance across layers without modifying model parameters.

A natural approach to both tasks is to embed passages using pretrained models and compare them via cosine similarity. While this paradigm is effective for high-resource languages, its behavior in historical languages such as Medieval Latin remains poorly understood. 
In particular, standard practice treats sentence representations as fixed outputs, without examining how semantic information is distributed across model depth or how representation extraction affects downstream performance. 
This raises a key question: to what extent do failures in semantic matching arise from limitations of the underlying model, as opposed to how its internal representations are used?


To answer this, we conduct a layerwise analysis of internal representations across a diverse set of models, including Latin-adapted encoders and multilingual models.
We uncover a striking and consistent failure mode. 
In Latin-adapted T5 encoders, standard mean-pooled hidden states undergo a pronounced mid-layer collapse, in which cosine similarity loses discriminative power and semantic separation degrades sharply, in some cases approaching chance. 
In contrast, multilingual encoders such as LaBSE and decoder-based models exhibit substantially more stable behavior across depth. 
This finding shows that model performance on Medieval Latin semantic matching depends critically on how representations are extracted, and that naïve pooling can obscure usable semantic information.



This observation leads to a key insight: the primary bottleneck is not missing semantic content, but the geometry of the representation space induced by common extraction methods. 
When representations become anisotropic or dominated by a few global directions, cosine similarity fails to distinguish semantically equivalent from non-equivalent passages, particularly in intermediate layers.



Motivated by this diagnosis, we apply a simple, train-free geometric correction based on All-but-the-Top (ABTT) post-processing~\cite{mu2018allbutthetop}. 
By removing dominant directions from the embedding space, this approach restores semantic separation and largely eliminates the mid-layer collapse.
The resulting representations are more stable across layers and yield consistently stronger performance on both duplicate detection and open-set semantic deduplication, without requiring any task-specific training.


Beyond improving matching performance, geometric correction also enhances interpretability. 
We find that applying integrated gradients on corrected representations produces clearer and more localized token-level attributions for semantic matches, whereas raw representations yield diffuse and less informative explanations.
This enables an inspectable, scholar-facing workflow in which retrieved matches can be both ranked and explained, supporting more reliable use of NLP tools in humanities research.

In summary, this paper makes the following contributions:
(1) we introduce a new benchmark for strict semantic matching in Medieval Latin canon law, including both pairwise and open-set formulations;
(2) we provide a layerwise analysis revealing a previously unreported mid-layer collapse in Latin-adapted T5 representations under standard pooling; and
(3) we show that a simple train-free geometric correction restores semantic separability across layers and models, and that the corrected representations yield sharper, more localized integrated-gradient attributions, supporting an inspectable semantic matching pipeline for humanities scholars.

\section{Task and Dataset}

\subsection{Task Definition}

We study two related tasks.

\paragraph{Task A: pairwise duplicate detection.} Let $p_1$ and $p_2$ be two passages and let $\equiv$ denote semantic equivalence, so that $p_1 \equiv p_2$ holds exactly when the two passages express the same meaning. Given an input pair $(p_1, p_2)$, the task is to predict
\[
y =
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } p_1 \equiv p_2, \\
0 & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]
The model produces $\cos(e_{p_1}, e_{p_2})$ from its pooled embeddings, with no access to directory structure or any threshold. Task A is evaluated only at the pairwise level on the test n$\times$n cosine matrix.

\paragraph{Task B: directory routing.} Task B operationalizes the same embeddings into the deployment workflow, which does require directory knowledge. Given a query passage $q$ and the existing labelled directories, the system either routes $q$ to one of those directories or flags it as new. We evaluate two variants of this routing decision. (i) \emph{Autonomous routing.} For each query we compute the maximum cosine to any other test file and compare it to a learned threshold $\tau$ (fit on the training split via the $F_1$-optimal cut on same-directory vs.\ different-directory pairs). If the score is above $\tau$ the query is routed to its top-1 neighbour's directory; otherwise it is flagged as new. (ii) \emph{Top-$k$ ranking.} The system scores each directory by the maximum cosine similarity between $q$ and any document already assigned to that directory, adds a pseudo-option \texttt{\_\_NEW\_\_} with score $\tau$, and presents the top-$k$ options to a human expert; we simulate that expert by assuming the labeled directory would be selected whenever it appears in the shortlist.

\subsection{Train/Test Split}

The corpus contains 1,705 medieval Latin manuscript fragments drawn from canon law and organized into 840 directories. Each directory corresponds to one original legal text, and the files inside it are manuscript copies that express the same content. Directory sizes range from 1 to 10 files.

We split the corpus into an 847/858 train/test set with a leak-free protocol (Table~\ref{tab:split}).\footnote{Implemented in \texttt{src/canon\_split\_v2.py}; deterministic with seed~42.} The 545 singleton directories are distributed roughly evenly between the two splits at the file level. Doubleton directories (108 in total) are assigned as whole folders, 54 to train and 54 to test, so that both files of each doubleton remain together; this contributes 54 within-directory positive training pairs that a file-level doubleton split would discard. For multi-file directories ($\geq$3 files, 187 directories), we vary the train allocation across folders within each size class: for a class of $K$ folders with directory size $n$, we distribute $\text{train}_n$ uniformly over $\{1,\ldots,n-1\}$ via stratified allocation, with the remainder weighted toward values nearest $n/2$. The class average remains balanced near 50/50, while every size class contributes same-folder positive pairs to training. This avoids the pathological case in which proportional splitting forces every size-3 directory to $(1\text{ train}, 2\text{ test})$ and contributes zero same-folder positives from that class. The redesign yields 565 positive training pairs, a roughly 60\% increase over the previous proportional split, and a more defensible setup in which no directory size class is structurally excluded from contributing same-folder training positives.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Models}

We evaluate six pre-trained models from three architecture families:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{LaTa}: a T5-based seq2seq model pre-trained on Latin \citep{riemenschneider2023lata}. We extract encoder hidden states (12 transformer blocks).
\item \textbf{PhilTa}: a philologically-oriented variant of LaTa, also T5-based \citep{riemenschneider2023lata}.
\item \textbf{LaBSE}: a language-agnostic BERT sentence encoder covering 109 languages \citep{feng2022labse} (12 encoder layers).
\item \textbf{mt5-base}: a multilingual T5 encoder-decoder \citep{xue2021mt5} (12 encoder layers).
\item \textbf{Qwen3-0.6B}: a multilingual decoder-only embedding model (28 layers) \citep{qwen3embedding2025}.
\item \textbf{KaLM-mini}: a multilingual decoder-only embedding model (24 layers) \citep{kalm2025embedding}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Embedding Extraction}

We extract hidden-state representations from every transformer block, starting at layer 1 and ending at the final block, while skipping layer 0, the static embedding table. LaTa, PhilTa, LaBSE, and mt5-base each contribute 12 layers. Qwen3-0.6B contributes 28, and KaLM-mini contributes 24. Token representations at each layer are then pooled into a single sentence vector, either by plain mean pooling or by SIF weighting. For the decoder-only models, last-token pooling is another natural choice; we compare it against mean pooling under ABTT post-processing across all six models in Appendix~\ref{app:lasttok}.

For the paper-facing pooling baseline, we remove only tokenizer artifacts with no lexical content, such as whitespace, padding, or tokenizer-prefix-only tokens. We keep punctuation and numerals in the pooled representations. In the explanation figures, though, we always show the original decoded tokens rather than filtered display tokens.

\subsection{Post-Processing: ABTT}

Our main post-processing method is All-but-the-Top (ABTT) \citep{mu2018allbutthetop}. We center the pooled sentence embeddings, compute the top-$D$ principal components by SVD on the training set, and project those components out of both train and test embeddings. At each layer we search $D \in \{1,2,3,5,7,10\}$ and keep the value that maximizes training-set directory accuracy at rank 1 in Task B. We refer to that as the \textbf{optimal} setting.

We also compare against two simpler alternatives. One is SIF-weighted pooling \citep{arora2017sif} without ABTT. The other is PCA whitening \citep{su2021whitening}. SIF assigns each token $w_i$ the weight $\alpha_i = a / (a + p(w_i))$, where $p(w_i)$ is the unigram probability estimated on the training set and $a = 10^{-3}$.

\subsection{Example-Level Attribution}

For the explanation figures, we use integrated gradients \citep{sundararajan2017axiomatic} as a local attribution method. Given a query passage and one candidate partner passage, we compute per-sequence token attributions toward pair cosine similarity. The displayed axes always use the original decoded tokens rather than filtered display tokens, even when a token's attribution is close to zero. The heatmap in Figure~\ref{fig:pairmatrix} is therefore a derived pairwise view: token-token cosine similarity is combined with the marginal integrated-gradient scores of the two sequences. We show that view before and after ABTT on the same example. We also render companion self-attention maps for the same pair as supporting context only, since ABTT is applied after encoding and does not change the model's internal attention weights.

We complement integrated gradients with a retrieval-aligned learned-mask attribution adapted from MaRC \citep{brinnerzarriess2023marc}. MaRC was proposed for classification: a soft mask $\mathbf{m} \in [0,1]^{T_q}$ over input tokens is optimized so that the masked forward preserves the target class logit. We adapt the same construction to retrieval, asking the mask to preserve the pair's cosine similarity instead.

Let $\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}_q) \in \mathbb{R}^{T_q \times d_{\text{emb}}}$ denote the input-embedding lookup, $E_{>0}$ the encoder with the lookup removed, and $\mathbf{z}_c^{\star}$ the ABTT-cleaned mean-pooled candidate, held fixed throughout optimization. We parameterize $\mathbf{m} = \sigma(\boldsymbol{\zeta})$ with free logits $\boldsymbol{\zeta} \in \mathbb{R}^{T_q}$ and write $\mathcal{I}$ for the content-token positions (attention-active, not tokenizer artifacts). The objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{m}) = \bigl(\cos(\mathrm{ABTT}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_q(\mathbf{m})), \mathbf{z}_c^{\star}) - \cos(\mathrm{ABTT}(\mathbf{z}_q), \mathbf{z}_c^{\star})\bigr)^2 + \lambda \!\!\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\!\! \frac{m_i}{|\mathcal{I}|} + \gamma\!\! \sum_{i,i+1\in\mathcal{I}} \!\! \frac{|m_{i+1}-m_i|}{|\mathcal{I}|-1},
\label{eq:retrieval_mark}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_q(\mathbf{m}) = \mathrm{pool}(E_{>0}(\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{m})\,\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}_q)), \mathbf{a}_q)$ is the masked pooled query, $(\lambda, \gamma)$ control sparsity and total-variation smoothness, and logits outside $\mathcal{I}$ are frozen at $-\infty$. We run 200 Adam steps per pair, initialize $\boldsymbol{\zeta}_0 = \log 9$ so that $\mathbf{m} \approx 0.9$ at the start, and report both a baseline variant that uses raw cosines in place of the ABTT-cleaned cosines in Equation~\ref{eq:retrieval_mark} and the ABTT variant stated above. The candidate-side mask is obtained by swapping the roles of query and candidate. We use this method alongside integrated gradients in the example-level analysis; quantitative faithfulness comparisons against other attribution methods are left as accompanying work.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

\paragraph{Task A.} Both Task~A metrics are properties of the test n$\times$n pairwise cosine matrix. \textbf{AUROC} treats every same-directory test pair as a positive and every different-directory pair as a negative, summarising how well cosine similarity ranks positives above negatives. \textbf{Cosine gap} is the difference between the mean same-directory and mean different-directory cosine, a direct measure of class separation. Neither uses a threshold and neither requires the model to know anything about directories.

\paragraph{Task B.} Task~B has two sets of metrics, one for each variant. For \emph{autonomous routing}, we report \textbf{overall assignment accuracy}: the rate at which the binary existing-vs-new decision matches ground truth, plus a correct directory match on files predicted existing. We decompose it into \textbf{existing accuracy} (restricted to files that have a same-directory partner in the test set) and \textbf{new accuracy} (restricted to files that should have been flagged as novel). For \emph{top-$k$ ranking}, we report \textbf{cumulative top-$K$ accuracy}: for each $K$ from 1 to 5, the fraction of test queries whose labeled directory appears among the model's top $K$ options. We also report \textbf{Acc@1} averaged over $M=5$ query/reference reseedings as a stable per-layer ranking signal. Training-set directory accuracy at rank~1 is the model-selection signal for ABTT's $D$.

\subsection{Cluster Geometry}

Cosine-gap and AUCROC curves summarize separability in aggregate. Two-dimensional projections make the geometric effect of ABTT visible directly. Figures~\ref{fig:tsne} and~\ref{fig:umap} show t-SNE~\citep{vandermaaten2008tsne} and UMAP~\citep{mcinnes2018umap} embeddings of the 1{,}705 labelled fragments at each model's best layer. Under whitening, highlighted fragments are scattered across the plane regardless of their directory; under baseline and SIF-only pooling, clusters form partially but colored points often split into separate sub-groups. The pattern holds across both projection algorithms, indicating that the tightening reflects the post-processed embedding geometry rather than an artifact of either dimensionality-reduction method. Silhouette scores computed in the 2D projection space on winnable fragments corroborate this: SIF+ABTT yields the highest score for every (model, projection) combination, while whitening collapses to values near $-0.5$ across the board.

\section{Discussion}

PCA whitening has worked well for English sentence similarity \citep{su2021whitening, huang2021whiteningbert}, but it performs poorly here. A plausible reason is that whitening removes variance that still helps separate equivalent from non-equivalent Latin legal passages. In this corpus, the original variance structure seems to carry useful signal rather than just noise.

The depth profiles also differ by architecture. The strongest dip appears in the two Latin T5 encoders. mt5-base shows a milder version of the same pattern, and LaBSE plus the decoder-only models vary much less across depth. Architecture and pre-training objective are the most likely reasons. After ABTT, those differences matter less in practice because the weak middle layers become much more usable. It also reduces the need to hunt for a narrow ``good'' layer band before retrieval.

We also tested SIF-weighted pooling and whitening as simpler alternatives. They can help in individual cases, but neither produces the same consistent repair across models and layers. Mean pooling plus ABTT remains the safer default for this retrieval pipeline.

The explanation component is intentionally local. The derived pair matrix and the companion attention map are there to help a scholar inspect one ranked option, not to summarize the model as a whole. That fits the human-in-the-loop directory-assignment setting much better than our earlier aggregate token-category analyses. We also ran the before-and-after ABTT comparison using five additional attribution methods beyond integrated gradients on the same example pairs, with consistent improvements in attribution clarity across all six models; for brevity we report only the IG pair-matrix view in the main text.


\section*{Limitations}

Our study covers one domain, medieval canon law, and one language, Latin. The 1,705-fragment corpus is large for this field, but it is still small by current NLP standards. We test six models, and models outside this set may behave differently. ABTT also needs enough unlabeled documents to estimate principal components well, and we have not tested it in settings with only a few hundred texts or fewer. The explanation analysis is local and example-based, and the human-in-the-loop evaluation is a simulation: we assume the labelled directory would be chosen whenever it appears in the model's shortlist, which captures ranking quality but not the judgment load of a working scholar.

Our example-level attributions use integrated gradients, with a retrieval-aligned learned-mask variant adapted from MaRC~\citep{brinnerzarriess2023marc} trained directly against the ABTT-cleaned cosine used for ranking as a complementary view. Both remain local explanations and do not summarize model behaviour globally, and both still depend on the choice of layer at which the hidden state is read. The joint two-sided optimization, in which a single mask is fit over both passages simultaneously rather than one side at a time, is left to future work, as is the quantitative faithfulness evaluation across attribution methods.
