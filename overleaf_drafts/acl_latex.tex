\begin{abstract}
Practitioners often read pre-trained encoders at intermediate layers, diagnose them with an anisotropy statistic, and correct them with a post-hoc projection.
These steps assume that the statistic tracks retrieval quality.
We test that assumption layer by layer on 1,705 medieval Latin canon-law witnesses, whose equivalence labels come from a scholarly edition.
In the middle layers of three T5 encoders, cosine retrieval falls to or near chance (AUROC 0.50--0.65), while three embedding-trained models stay above 0.80 at every layer.
Mean pairwise cosine gives no warning.
Top-PC share, the first principal component's variance share, flags all 26 collapsed layers with one false alarm in 100, but orders layers only weakly within each group.
A top-$D$ projection fit on training vectors lifts every layer to AUROC 0.96--0.99 and cuts the cross-model spread in routing accuracy from 39.3 to 3.3 points.
The repair matches, but does not exceed, a character n-gram reference.
Contrastive fine-tuning of LaTa improves its last layer but not its collapsed middle layers.
At the collapsed layers one direction holds most of the variance, but removing it alone restores only part of the ranking; removing two to five restores nearly all of it.
We test whether a few massive, passage-varying residual coordinates cause the collapse, and whether T5 pretraining or the missing embedding objective produces it \pending{E1, E2, P2x2: one-sentence result}.
We release the frozen testbed and split, the evaluation code, and lexical reference scores.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Pre-trained encoders are a standard representation for semantic matching in text-reuse and digital-humanities pipelines \citep[e.g.,][]{manjavacas2019allusive, schelb2026locisimiles, reichbauer2026alignment}.
Practitioners take a model off the shelf and often read an intermediate layer, since intermediate layers can outperform the final one \citep{skean2025layer}.
They diagnose the embedding space with an anisotropy statistic, usually the mean cosine between passages, adapted from the word-level statistic of \citet{ethayarajh2019contextual}.
They then apply a post-hoc correction, such as removing the top principal directions \citep{mu2018allbutthetop} or whitening \citep{su2021whitening}.
The last two steps assume that the statistic tracks retrieval quality: a layer the statistic calls healthy should retrieve well, and a layer it flags should gain from the correction.
We test that assumption layer by layer.

Medieval canon law gives a controlled case for this test.
Before the twelfth century it survives in many compilations, whose witnesses differ in spelling, wording, and fragment boundaries.
Matching a witness to its source by hand is labor-intensive expert work, because scribes often omitted or misnamed the source.
The Carolingian Canon Law project \citep[CCL;][]{firey2009ccl} has already made these decisions for 1,705 witnesses of 840 sources.
Its labels are expert-assigned by the project and checked by collation in its parallel display, so they are independent of any model.
A character n-gram reference retrieves at least as well as every pre-trained encoder configuration we test, so the equivalence signal is present in the surface text.
A retrieval failure therefore points to the representation rather than to the data.
% If P2x2 does not land, drop the next four sentences (the matched comparison rests on it).
Latin also has dedicated encoders in two architectures.
The T5 models LaTa and PhilTa have encoder-only siblings, LaBERTa and PhilBERTa, trained by the same authors on the same data \citep{riemenschneider2023lata}.
Latin BERT \citep{bamman2020latinbert} is a further encoder-only model.
This allows a matched comparison of architectures (Section~\ref{sec:objective}).

The layerwise view shows a failure that the usual diagnostic misses.
In the middle layers of the three T5 encoders in our panel, cosine retrieval falls to or near chance: pairwise AUROC at the worst layer is 0.50 for LaTa, 0.54 for PhilTa, and 0.65 for mT5-base.
The three embedding-trained models stay above 0.80 at every layer.
Mean pairwise cosine gives no warning.
It is highest in the models that retrieve best, and within the T5 encoders it is lower at the collapsed layers than at the healthy ones.
Variance statistics do better.
A top-PC share of 0.6 or more marks all 26 collapsed layers, with one false alarm in 100 model-layers.
Within a regime, however, top-PC share and effective rank order layers only weakly (Section~\ref{sec:geometry}).
A top-$D$ projection fit on training vectors \citep[ABTT;][]{mu2018allbutthetop}, with no parameter update, lifts every layer of every model to AUROC 0.96 to 0.99.
It also compresses the spread in routing accuracy across models from 39.3 to 3.3 points.
The repair matches the n-gram reference and does not exceed it.
Contrastive fine-tuning raises LaTa's last layer from 0.938 to 0.984 AUROC, yet its layers 2 to 11 stay at 0.50 to 0.57.

Prior work shows that a few residual coordinates of very large magnitude can dominate cosine similarity \citep{timkey2021rogue}.
In decoder language models they persist through the middle layers \citep{sun2024massive}, and in T5 encoders they grow with depth \citep{zhao2025outlier}.
We test whether mean pooling turns these coordinates into values that vary from passage to passage and so drown out content (Section~\ref{sec:localizing}).
We also test whether T5 pretraining or the missing embedding objective produces the collapse (Section~\ref{sec:objective}).
A label-free check finds the same low-rank middle layers on English court opinions, including in a T5 encoder pretrained on English alone.

We measure the geometry on two tasks.
Pairwise retrieval (Task~A) scores every pair of witnesses and tests ranking directly.
Open-set routing (Task~B) files each witness under a known source or flags it as new, which adds a threshold decision.
The embedding-trained models show a second regime.
In them every passage shares a large mean vector, which raises every cosine (mean pairwise cosine 0.59 to 0.97), while variance stays spread over many directions.
These models rank well, yet the projection still raises the share of witnesses LaBSE files correctly as known or new from 83.3 to 90.8 percent.
Section~\ref{sec:repair} shows that this gain holds without any threshold and does not come from the shared mean vector.

This paper makes four contributions.
(1)~We give the first per-layer retrieval profile of T5 encoder states in isolation that we know of: in our panel, T5 encoders collapse to or near chance at mid-depth. A $2 \times 2$ control crosses T5 pretraining with an embedding objective \pending{P2x2: raw encoder-only and embedding-trained T5 cells}.
(2)~We compare anisotropy statistics with retrieval layer by layer. Top-PC share and effective rank separate collapsed from healthy layers, whereas mean pairwise cosine is lower at collapsed layers than at healthy ones. A train-only projection restores every layer, which shows that the signal was present in the layer but masked.
(3)~We give a falsifiable test of the massive-coordinate account for mean-pooled passage retrieval (coordinate ablation, token audit, subspace split, and a $D$ ablation), and find that the nuisance spans two to five directions, which rank below the retained directions in every pre-trained model \pending{E1, E2: coordinate ablation and token audit result}.
(4)~We release the frozen testbed and split, the evaluation code, and lexical reference scores (Section~\ref{sec:testbed}).

\section{Testbed and Protocol}
\label{sec:testbed}

\paragraph{Corpus.}
\label{sec:dataset}
The Carolingian Canon Law project \citep[CCL;][]{firey2009ccl} is an open-access edition of early medieval, mostly ninth-century, canon-law manuscripts that keys each transcribed unit to the source it copies.
A \emph{directory} is one source, and a \emph{witness} is one manuscript's copy of it; the testbed holds 1,705 witnesses in 840 directories.
The labels are expert-assigned by the project and checked by collation in its parallel display (Appendix~\ref{app:corpus_provenance}), so no model shaped them.
The material is licensed CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 with attribution to the project.
We will release the frozen testbed and split under the same license, in coordination with the project's versioned exports, together with the evaluation code and lexical reference scores.

\paragraph{Split.}
We divide the witnesses into 847 training and 858 test files, and pairs form only within a split.
Singleton directories (545 of 840) are divided file by file, doubletons go whole to one side, and larger directories are divided internally so that every size class yields training positives.
This gives 565 positive training and 596 positive test pairs; 535 test files have a same-directory partner in test (\emph{existing}) and 323 do not (\emph{new}).

\paragraph{Tasks and metrics.}
\label{sec:tasks}
Task~A scores every pair of test witnesses by the cosine of their pooled embeddings, and we report AUROC with same-directory pairs as positives.
Task~B is open-set routing: file an unidentified witness under a known directory, or flag it as new.
Each test file scores every test directory by its maximum cosine to a member.
A pseudo-directory \texttt{NEW} scores the threshold $\tau$.
The top-scoring entry is the decision.
Directory accuracy at rank~1 (DirAcc@1) requires that entry to be the labeled directory, or \texttt{NEW} for a new file.
Assignment accuracy scores only the existing-versus-new decision.
Labeling every file as existing already scores 62.4\%, so we report assignment accuracy alongside DirAcc@1.

Per layer we also read five statistics: three label-free geometry statistics and two label-based separation indices.
The \emph{mean pairwise cosine} over all pairs of pooled test passages is the usual anisotropy statistic, adapted from the word-level statistic of \citet{ethayarajh2019contextual}, which uses random pairs.
Let $p_i = \lambda_i / \sum_j \lambda_j$ for the covariance eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \dots$ of the centered training embeddings.
The \emph{top-PC share} (the share of centered variance on the first principal component) is $p_1$.
The \emph{effective rank} is $\exp(-\sum_i p_i \log p_i)$, the entropy effective rank of \citet{roy2007effectiverank} applied to the covariance eigenvalues; it is 1 when one direction holds all variance.
The \emph{cosine gap} $g$ is the mean same-directory minus the mean different-directory test cosine, and the \emph{standardized gap} $g/s$ divides it by the standard deviation $s$ of all pairwise test cosines, putting models with different cosine spreads on one scale.
The two gaps need labels.

\paragraph{Train-only rule.}
\label{sec:setup}
\label{sec:layers}
A headline cell reads each model and setting at the layer with the highest training AUROC for Task~A, or training DirAcc@1 for Task~B (Table~\ref{tab:selected_layers}).
The threshold $\tau$ maximizes training F1 over same- versus different-directory pairs.
SIF frequencies are fit on training texts, and ABTT components and $D$ on training embeddings.
Layerwise profiles show every layer and select nothing.
The rule selects every layer, threshold, and projection from training data alone.
Analysis quantities (the collapse cutoff and the worst layers) use test AUROC and select no reported headline cell.
Appendix~\ref{app:attribution} uses a predeclared variant of the rule for attribution layers.

\paragraph{Post-processing.}
\label{sec:postproc}
We mean-pool the hidden states of every transformer block, dropping only tokenizer artifacts without lexical content.
All-but-the-Top \citep[ABTT;][]{mu2018allbutthetop} subtracts the training mean and removes the top $D$ principal components of the centered training embeddings from all vectors, with $D \in \{1,2,3,5,7,10\}$ chosen per layer by training DirAcc@1.
We write ABTT-only when we contrast it with SIF+ABTT.
SIF \citep{arora2017sif} instead reweights each token by $a/(a+p(w))$ before pooling ($a=10^{-3}$), with $p(w)$ estimated on training texts, and gives special tokens, such as the end-of-sequence token, zero weight.
We use only this weighting step of SIF, not its common-component removal.
SIF+ABTT applies both.
Full-rank PCA whitening \citep{su2021whitening} is ill-conditioned here, since it estimates 768 to 1,024 dimensions from 847 training vectors.
It degenerates the learned threshold and also ranks below ABTT at every layer, at Task~A AUROC 0.82--0.88 for the T5 encoders and LaBSE and 0.55--0.64 for the two decoders, so we exclude it (Appendix~\ref{app:sif_variants}).
Whitening to a reduced dimension, as \citet{su2021whitening} also do, is the fairer comparison.
Fit on training embeddings with $k=128$ or $256$ components, it ranks on par with ABTT (Table~\ref{tab:whiten_reduced}, Appendix~\ref{app:sif_variants}): Task~A AUROC 0.973--0.984 at each model's train-selected layer against 0.971--0.987, and within 0.021 of ABTT at every one of the 100 model-layers, collapsed layers included; $k=64$ falls slightly below.
On pairwise ranking the repair therefore does not depend on ABTT in particular.
On routing it does: reduced-rank whitening does not degenerate the threshold as full rank does, but at each model's train-selected Task~B layer $k=128$ reaches DirAcc@1 1.9 to 4.5 points below ABTT, and $k=256$ up to 15 points below.
We therefore keep ABTT as the reference.

\paragraph{Model panel.}
Table~\ref{tab:model_panel} crosses two factors.
The first is the architecture with its pretraining objective: a T5 encoder with span-corruption pretraining, or an encoder-only model with masked-language-model pretraining.
The second is whether a contrastive embedding objective follows pretraining.
The T5 encoders we have run are the Latin LaTa, its trilingual sibling PhilTa \citep{riemenschneider2023lata}, and mT5-base \citep{xue2021mt5}.
The controls we have run are LaBSE \citep{feng2022labse}, Qwen3-0.6B \citep{qwen3embedding2025}, and KaLM-mini \citep{kalm2025embedding}.
In the models run so far the two factors are confounded: every T5 encoder is raw and every control is embedding-trained, so a T5-only collapse could reflect either.
The pending rows break the confound.
LaBERTa and PhilBERTa share authors and pretraining data with LaTa and PhilTa \citep{riemenschneider2023lata}, and Latin BERT \citep{bamman2020latinbert} is a second raw Latin encoder.
Sentence-T5 \citep{ni2022sentencet5} fills the embedding-trained T5 cell, and T5-v1.1-base \citep{raffel2020t5} is its matched raw partner.

\paragraph{Lexical reference.}
Character 3--5-gram TF-IDF, fit on training files, reaches 0.987 AUROC on Task~A, tying the best pre-trained encoder cell, and 89.9 DirAcc@1 on Task~B, level with the best pre-trained ones.
In every tercile of n-gram overlap among positive test pairs (terciles cut on the TF-IDF score itself), it ranks a witness's partner first at least as often as every baseline and ABTT encoder configuration at its train-selected layer.
The equivalence signal is therefore present in the surface text.
Our claims concern where the retrieval signal sits inside encoders and what masks it, not whether embeddings beat n-grams on this corpus.
Table~\ref{tab:lexical_baselines} (Appendix~\ref{app:reference_systems}) gives all three lexical scorers.

\section{Two Geometries}
\label{sec:geometry}

This section asks whether the anisotropy statistic that practitioners read tracks retrieval layer by layer.
We first give the retrieval profile of every model, then set the geometry statistics of Section~\ref{sec:testbed} against it.

\paragraph{Retrieval by depth.}
Figure~\ref{fig:dip} plots test AUROC against depth for all six models.
The three T5 encoders fall to or near chance in their middle layers.
Baseline AUROC bottoms out at 0.496 for LaTa at layer 6, at 0.538 for PhilTa at layer 10, and at 0.654 for mT5-base at layer 5.
We call a layer \emph{collapsed} when its baseline AUROC is below 0.70.
By this definition, 26 of the 36 T5 layers collapse: layers 2--11 of LaTa, 3--11 of PhilTa, and 5--11 of mT5-base.
We set this cutoff after inspecting test AUROC; it describes the profiles and selects no headline cell.
Each T5 encoder recovers at its last layer, where LaTa reaches 0.938.
The three embedding-trained models never fall below 0.806, and they improve broadly with depth to a best layer of 0.960 to 0.972.
In our panel the collapse is thus confined to the T5 encoders, which are also the only models without an embedding objective (Section~\ref{sec:objective}).
ABTT, fit on training embeddings, restores every layer of every model (Section~\ref{sec:repair}).
The question is whether a geometry statistic would have flagged the collapsed layers.

\paragraph{Mean pairwise cosine.}
The usual diagnostic gives no warning of the collapse.
Table~\ref{tab:geometry_regimes} summarizes each model's geometry over its layers.
Mean pairwise cosine, averaged over all pairs of test passages, is highest in the embedding-trained models.
Their mean cosine ranges from 0.585 to 0.974 across layers, while their retrieval stays above 0.80.
At the collapsed T5 layers the statistic ranges from 0.23 to 0.62.
LaTa (0.23--0.30) and mT5-base (0.30--0.36) sit below every embedding-trained layer, and PhilTa (0.53--0.62) overlaps the bottom of that range.
Within the T5 encoders the statistic runs against the usual reading.
Collapsed layers have lower mean cosine than healthy ones, so it correlates with AUROC at Spearman $+0.67$ (Table~\ref{tab:geometry_correlations}).
Within the embedding-trained models it carries almost no information (Spearman $+0.10$, Pearson $-0.06$).
Computing it on training passages instead gives the same picture (Spearman $+0.72$ within T5, $+0.12$ within the embedding-trained models).
A practitioner who reads a high mean cosine as a warning would flag the healthy models and pass the collapsed layers.

\paragraph{Variance statistics.}
The variance statistics separate the two groups, with one exception (mT5-base layer~4).
We compute top-PC share and effective rank on centered training embeddings, so the shared offset that drives mean cosine does not enter them.
At the collapsed T5 layers the top principal component holds 76 to 100 percent of the variance, and effective rank lies between 1.0 and 4.6.
The two transition layers at the onset of collapse, LaTa layer 2 and PhilTa layer 3, set the lower end of the share and the upper end of the rank.
Without them the ranges narrow to 83--100 percent and 1.00--2.17.
At mT5-base layers 5--11 one direction holds all the variance to three decimals.
In the embedding-trained models the largest top-PC share at any layer is 0.18 for Qwen3-0.6B, 0.42 for KaLM-mini, and 0.52 for LaBSE.
Their effective rank ranges from 16 to 150.
ABTT with $D=10$ removes the concentration.
On the training vectors it is fit on, this follows by construction.
On test passages at the collapsed layers, top-PC share falls to 0.03--0.05 and effective rank rises to 148--173.

\paragraph{What the statistics predict.}
Figure~\ref{fig:geometry_scatter} sets both kinds of statistic against retrieval over all 100 model-layers, and Table~\ref{tab:geometry_correlations} gives the correlations.
Across the panel, top-PC share correlates with baseline AUROC at Spearman $-0.77$ and effective rank at $+0.77$, against $+0.55$ for mean pairwise cosine.
Both coefficients are inflated by the two clusters of collapsed and healthy layers visible in panel~(a), and the Pearson value of $-0.92$ most of all.
Layers within a model are also not independent, so the 100 model-layers carry the evidence of roughly six clusters.
Leaving out any one model keeps the top-PC Spearman between $-0.70$ and $-0.83$; this shows robustness to single models, not to the clustering.
A threshold states the separation more directly.
A top-PC share of 0.6 or more marks all 26 collapsed layers with one false alarm, mT5-base layer~4, and every cutoff from 0.53 to 0.76 gives the same split.
Like the collapse cutoff, this threshold is descriptive and was read off the data.
Within a family, the variance statistics track AUROC only weakly.
Top-PC share gives Spearman $-0.50$ within the T5 encoders and $-0.43$ within the embedding-trained models; effective rank gives $+0.53$ and $+0.40$.
Within single models, the top-PC Spearman runs from $-0.28$ (LaBSE) to $-0.94$ (mT5-base) over 12 to 28 layers.
mT5-base layer~4 shows the limit.
Its top-PC share is 0.893 and its effective rank 2.15, yet its AUROC of 0.799 is close to that of its healthy layers 1--3.
The lower block of Table~\ref{tab:geometry_correlations} adds no separate evidence.
ABTT lifts every layer into a narrow band (0.962 to 0.987), so its gain is nearly a constant minus the baseline, and the gain correlations mirror the baseline ones.
The variance statistics thus separate the collapsed regime from the healthy one.
They do not rank layers within a regime, and a high share alone does not guarantee collapse.
This limit fits reports that geometric metrics of the final-layer unembeddings of OLMo-style language models do not reliably predict their downstream performance \citep{kulkarni2026geometry} and that the cosine geometry of sentence embeddings does not predict their task performance \citep{nastase2025geometry}.

\paragraph{The standardized gap.}
The raw cosine gap, the margin a threshold works with, tracks retrieval even less well.
Across the panel it correlates with AUROC at Spearman $+0.22$.
The standardized gap reaches $+0.97$, and for the AUROC target every Spearman column of Table~\ref{tab:geometry_correlations} keeps it between $+0.93$ and $+0.98$.
This agreement is expected by construction.
The standardized gap is a label-based signal-to-noise ratio, close to the $d'$ from which a binormal model computes AUROC, so we do not count it as a predictor.
Its use here is to dissolve the mT5-base paradox.
mT5-base shows why the two gaps differ (Figure~\ref{fig:standardized_gap}, Appendix~\ref{app:layer_diagnostics}).
Its raw gap rises from about 0.03 to 0.21--0.25 exactly where ranking fails, and within the model it correlates with AUROC at $-0.78$.
Over the same layers, the standard deviation of all pairwise cosines grows from 0.05--0.07 at layers 1--3 to about 0.75.
Divided by that spread, the gap falls to 0.28--0.33, below the 0.41--0.49 of layers 1--3 and the 0.78 of layer 12.
The within-model correlation then turns to $+0.52$.
A gap that rises as ranking fails is thus no paradox: the collapsed layers spread all cosines widely, and same-directory pairs gain little relative to that spread.
The resolution is partial.
At mT5-base layer~4 the standardized gap is the lowest of the model (0.19), yet AUROC is 0.80.
LaTa adds an anomaly that the standardized gap does not explain.
Its raw gap is negative at layers 3--11, between $-0.05$ and $-0.07$, so same-directory pairs score slightly below different-directory pairs on average.
We do not have an account of this yet, and the token audit of Section~\ref{sec:localizing} tests one candidate (E2).

\paragraph{SIF at the collapsed layers.}
SIF offers a first hint about what feeds the dominant direction.
It down-weights frequent tokens and drops special tokens before pooling (Section~\ref{sec:postproc}), and its effect at the collapsed layers splits the T5 encoders.
It lifts LaTa from 0.50--0.56 to 0.84--0.94 and PhilTa from 0.54--0.60 to 0.87--0.93.
It moves mT5-base only from 0.65--0.66 to 0.67--0.69.
ABTT reaches 0.96--0.98 at the same layers in all three.
This pattern suggests that tokens SIF down-weights or drops carry the dominant direction in LaTa and PhilTa, and that tokens it keeps carry it in mT5-base.
Because SIF differs from mean pooling in two ways, the token audit of Section~\ref{sec:localizing} separates them (E2).

\paragraph{Two regimes.}
The panel therefore shows two regimes.
In the embedding-trained models every passage shares a large mean vector, which raises every cosine, and variance spreads over many directions.
These models rank well but still gain from the projection.
Their baseline AUROC is 0.806 to 0.972, and ABTT adds 0.007 to 0.031 at their respective train-selected layers, a gain within bootstrap noise for Qwen3-0.6B and KaLM-mini (Section~\ref{sec:repair}).
In the middle layers of the T5 encoders one direction takes most of the variance, and cosine ranking falls to or near chance.
The cosines there are spread across both signs rather than moderate.
At mT5-base's collapsed layers the 5th percentile of test cosines lies between $-0.83$ and $-0.77$, the median between 0.84 and 0.90, and the standard deviation about 0.75.
At LaTa's collapsed layers the 5th percentile lies between $-0.50$ and $-0.26$, and the standard deviation reaches 0.63.
Prior work already separates these two components.
\citet{timkey2021rogue} distinguish a constant offset from a few high-variance dimensions that dominate cosine, and IsoScore \citep{rudman2022isoscore} shows that the mean cosine of random pairs mainly measures the offset.
For final-layer embeddings, top-dimension variance share predicts cosine failure better than mean cosine \citep{parupudi2026anisotropy}, and effective rank is the most informative geometric predictor of retrieval quality across many models, though the prediction is moderate \citep{rottach2025topology}.
Our contribution is the layerwise version of this comparison within one testbed, including raw T5 encoder states, where the low-rank regime coincides with chance retrieval.
Section~\ref{sec:localizing} asks what produces that dominant direction.

\section{Localizing the Collapse}
\label{sec:localizing}

Section~\ref{sec:geometry} showed that collapsed T5 layers concentrate their centered variance on one direction.
Their cosines also take both signs, so the pooled vectors lie on both sides of one axis.
The projection on the dominant direction therefore changes sign across passages, which a shift shared by all passages cannot produce.
Prior work on outlier dimensions offers a candidate account.
In pre-trained transformers a few residual coordinates reach magnitudes far above the median \citep{kovaleva2021bertbusters,puccetti2022outlier} and dominate cosine similarity \citep{timkey2021rogue}.
In decoder language models these massive activations take near-constant values on the tokens that carry them \citep{sun2024massive}, and T5 encoders carry such outliers too \citep{zhao2025outlier}.
Their prevalence tracks token frequency in pretraining \citep{puccetti2022outlier}.
Mean pooling adds one step.
It averages the per-token values over a token mix that differs from passage to passage, so the pooled coordinate varies across passages.
If that variation is large, cosine ranks passages by one coordinate and ignores their content.

Preliminary evidence fits this account.
At LaTa layer 7, the layer the attribution rule selects (Appendix~\ref{app:attribution}), three coordinates have a mean magnitude of about 5,000 against a median of 36.
Their share of the pooled cosine, measured as the summed per-coordinate contribution of \citet{timkey2021rogue}, is \pendingnum{E1: LaTa layer 7 share, to recompute}.
Massive coordinates alone do not collapse retrieval, however.
At mT5-base layer 1, three coordinates carry \pendingnum{E1: mT5-base layer 1 share, to recompute} of the cosine, yet AUROC is 0.82 and top-PC share is 0.23.
The refined hypothesis is that collapse occurs when the pooled massive coordinates vary across passages at least as much as they shift all passages together.
We make this measurable with $r$, the standard deviation of a pooled coordinate across training passages divided by the absolute value of its mean.
The hypothesis predicts $r \geq 1$ for the top coordinates at collapsed T5 layers, and $r < 1$ at mT5-base layer~1 and in Qwen3-0.6B.
A high centered top-PC share already means that one direction varies across passages.
The testable content is that this direction is aligned with a few coordinates and has token carriers.
The four tests below each state a prediction and its alternative.
Every intervention is fit on training embeddings only.

\paragraph{Coordinate ablation (E1).}
Zeroing a single outlier dimension already improves cross-lingual retrieval in a multilingual encoder \citep{haemmerl2023anisotropy}.
We test whether such coordinates explain a collapse to chance, and at which depths.
At every layer of LaTa, PhilTa, mT5-base, LaBSE and Qwen3-0.6B, we rank residual coordinates two ways on training passages: by mean absolute value, and by variance across passages.
For each ranking we zero the top $k$ coordinates, $k \in \{1,3,5,10\}$.
We also standardize every coordinate by its training mean and standard deviation \citep{timkey2021rogue}, and we center the vectors without removing any component ($D=0$).
ABTT with $D=1$ serves as a reference, since it repairs a rank-one nuisance under either account, and ABTT with $D=3$ as a second, since it recovers at least 80 percent of the $D=10$ gain at every collapsed T5 layer.
If a few passage-varying coordinates cause the collapse, zeroing the top $k \le 5$ coordinates by variance restores AUROC at the collapsed T5 layers to at least 0.90.
Zeroing by mean magnitude, which also picks coordinates that shift all passages together, should help less.
Top-PC share then falls toward the range of the non-T5 models.
For LaBSE and Qwen3-0.6B, which have no collapsed layer, zeroing should change AUROC by at most 0.03 at their train-selected layers, where ABTT itself adds 0.007 to 0.031, and should recover only a small part of the ABTT gain at their weakest layers.
If standardization repairs a collapsed layer but zeroing $k \le 5$ does not, the nuisance sits on a few coordinates, but more than five.
If neither repairs it and only a projection does, the dominant axis is spread over many coordinates of similar variance.
The account then reduces to ``a few dominant directions'', which is what Section~\ref{sec:geometry} and the $D$ ablation below already report.
Centering alone tests whether the shared offset matters at all at these layers.
Table~\ref{tab:e1_coordinate_ablation} gives each model at its worst baseline layer.

\paragraph{Token audit (E2).}
The account names a carrier: some tokens hold the massive values.
For each collapsed T5 layer we record which tokens carry the top coordinates, their sign and magnitude, and the ratio $r$ of the pooled coordinates.
We also relate each passage's score on the top component to its log length and to its share of the 100 most frequent tokens.
SIF sharpens the test.
It rescues LaTa and PhilTa but not mT5-base (Section~\ref{sec:geometry}), and it differs from mean pooling in two ways: it down-weights frequent tokens, and it drops special tokens such as the end-of-sequence token.
A control separates the two: mean pooling with special tokens dropped and no frequency weighting.
If that control alone rescues LaTa and PhilTa, the end-of-sequence token carries their coordinates; if only full SIF does, frequent tokens do.
For mT5-base the evidence points away from the end-of-sequence token.
If that token carried its coordinates, SIF would delete it and rescue mT5-base.
Since SIF does not, mT5-base's carriers must be tokens that SIF keeps.
If frequent tokens carried the coordinates in all three models, the SIF split would need another explanation, and the frequency link would weaken.
The same audit on Qwen3-0.6B tests the refined hypothesis.
Massive coordinates there should shift all passages together ($r < 1$).
If they instead vary across passages as much as in the T5s, the refinement is false.
The audit also addresses LaTa's negative raw gap: same-directory pairs at its middle layers score slightly lower than different-directory pairs.
If the top component tracks passage length, and same-directory witnesses differ in length more than different-directory pairs do, this gap follows.
Comparing the absolute difference in log length between the two kinds of pair checks the second condition directly.
We find \pending{E2: carrier tokens per model and layer; ratio $r$ of the pooled coordinates; AUROC of mean pooling without special tokens; correlation of the top-component score with log length and frequent-token share; expected: frequent tokens carry the coordinates in LaTa and PhilTa, tokens that SIF keeps carry them in mT5-base, and Qwen3-0.6B coordinates have $r < 1$}.

\paragraph{Subspace split (E3).}
Our account says that the top $D$ directions ABTT removes hold nuisance in pre-trained spaces.
We rank passages by their projection onto PC1 alone, onto PCs 2 to $D$, and onto the retained subspace, and we compare the removed with the retained subspace.
At collapsed layers the full vector already ranks at chance and the removed subspace dominates it, so the test informs mainly where the full vector is healthy.
The cleanest contrast is LaTa's layer 12, which the pre-trained and fine-tuned models share.
ABTT raises the pre-trained layer from 0.938 to 0.971 and lowers the fine-tuned one from 0.984 to 0.970 in point estimate.
If the removed subspace holds nuisance in pre-trained spaces, it ranks well below the retained subspace there, although PCs 2 to $D$ may still carry some topical signal.
Fine-tuning is reported to concentrate task signal in the dominant directions \citep{rajaee2021finetuning}.
If that holds here, the removed subspace of fine-tuned LaTa, Qwen3-0.6B and KaLM-mini ranks well above chance and close to the retained one.
This would explain the drop in fine-tuned LaTa's AUROC under ABTT.
If the removed subspace ranks no better after fine-tuning than before, that drop needs another cause.
We find that the removed subspace ranks below the retained one in every pre-trained model (Table~\ref{tab:e3_subspace_split}, Appendix~\ref{app:abtt_ablations}).
At the 26 collapsed T5 layers it ranks at chance (median AUROC 0.489; PC1 alone 0.463), while the retained subspace reaches 0.977.
Where the full vector is healthy the removed subspace still carries retrieval signal, mostly in PCs 2 to $D$, but the retained subspace leads by at least 0.08 in the T5 encoders and 0.04 in the other models at every pre-trained layer.
The third outcome that would falsify the account (Scope, below) therefore does not occur.
At the selected layers of the pre-trained models the top $D$ directions also rank below the next $D$ (LaTa layer 12: 0.868 against 0.907), so per dimension they carry less signal than what follows them; after fine-tuning they rank above it (0.953 against 0.900 for LaTa).
At LaTa's layer 12 the pre-trained gain from ABTT comes from centering alone (0.972), so removing the top components changes AUROC by $-0.001$ before fine-tuning and $-0.015$ after it, in point estimate.
In fine-tuned Qwen3-0.6B and KaLM-mini the removed subspace also comes within 0.02 of the retained one (0.983 against 0.992, and 0.978 against 0.994, at $D=10$).
This is consistent with fine-tuning moving task signal into the dominant directions \citep{rajaee2021finetuning}, and with the point-estimate drop of fine-tuned LaTa's AUROC under ABTT from 0.984 to 0.970.

\paragraph{How many directions (H1).}
ABTT selects $D=10$, the top of the searched grid, in 87 of 100 model-layers (81 of 100 when SIF precedes it).
Selection by training DirAcc@1 never picks $D \le 2$: it picks $D=3$ once, $D=5$ twice, and $D=7$ ten times.
This already sits uneasily with a single nuisance axis, at least for routing.
We therefore sweep $D \in \{0,1,2,3,5,7,10,15,20,30,50\}$ at all 100 model-layers, where $D=0$ is centering alone, and read Task~A AUROC.
If the nuisance is rank-1, $D=1$ recovers most of the AUROC gain at the collapsed T5 layers.
We set the threshold at 80 percent of the $D=10$ gain.
The curve then stays flat beyond $D=1$, and centering alone recovers little.
If $D=1$ falls below that threshold, the nuisance is a low-dimensional subspace, and we drop the one-direction wording.
Figure~\ref{fig:d_ablation} shows the sweep.
We find that the nuisance is not rank-1.
At the 26 collapsed T5 layers, $D=1$ recovers a median 45 percent of the $D=10$ AUROC gain (13 to 89 percent) and reaches the 80 percent threshold at only one layer, LaTa layer~2.
Centering alone recovers nothing (median $-4$ percent).
Two components recover a median 78 percent, and three recover at least 80 percent at every collapsed layer (median 96 percent, AUROC 0.964 against 0.977 at $D=10$; Table~\ref{tab:d_ablation}, Appendix~\ref{app:abtt_ablations}).
This happens although the first component holds a median 94 percent of the centered training variance at these layers: one direction dominates the variance, but the nuisance that breaks cosine ranking spans a few directions, two to three in LaTa and PhilTa and more in mT5-base.
$D=1$ recovers the most in mT5-base (median 64 percent) and the least in PhilTa (14 percent), but mT5-base needs the most components overall: LaTa reaches the full gain with two (median 98 percent), PhilTa with three (95 percent), while mT5-base still lacks 15 percent at $D=3$ and keeps rising until $D=10$.
The failure of $D=1$ is not an estimation artifact: at LaTa layer~6, PhilTa layer~10 and mT5-base layer~8 the first component fit on training and on test passages agree to $|\cos| \ge 0.995$, removing a test-fitted first component also leaves AUROC at 0.684, 0.666 and 0.869, and after the training-fitted removal the next direction still holds 66 to 74 percent of the remaining test variance in LaTa and PhilTa.
Beyond $D=10$ the curve is flat to within about 0.02, and extending the selection grid to $D=50$ would move training selection to $D \ge 15$ at 99 of 100 model-layers while changing test AUROC by at most 0.023.
We therefore drop the one-direction wording for what breaks ranking.

\paragraph{Scope.}
We do not ask where these coordinates come from in pretraining, or why they peak at mid-depth in the T5 encoders of our panel.
We ask only whether they explain the retrieval failure.
Three outcomes would falsify the account.
First, neither zeroing $k \le 5$ coordinates nor standardization repairs the collapsed layers while ABTT does, which makes the nuisance a distributed direction.
Second, Qwen3-0.6B shows passage-varying massive coordinates ($r \geq 1$) of T5 scale without collapse.
Third, the removed subspace ranks as well as the retained one in the pre-trained T5s, which means ABTT discards signal along with nuisance.
Under any of these, the paper's claim reduces to the geometric description of Section~\ref{sec:geometry} and the repair.
Section~\ref{sec:objective} turns from the coordinates to a second question: whether T5 pretraining or the missing embedding objective produces them.

\section{Objective or Architecture?}
\label{sec:objective}

Our panel confounds two factors.
Every T5 we have run is a raw pre-trained encoder.
Every model that does not collapse is embedding-trained: LaBSE, Qwen3-0.6B and KaLM-mini.
The collapse may follow T5 pretraining, or it may follow the absence of an embedding objective.
The panel so far cannot separate the two, so the claim that collapse is T5-specific holds only in our panel.

Table~\ref{tab:model_panel} gives the design.
The LaTa--LaBERTa and PhilTa--PhilBERTa pairs hold data and authors fixed and vary the architecture factor.
The embedding-trained T5 cell holds Sentence-T5 (or GTR-T5) \citep{ni2022sentencet5,ni2022gtr}.
Its matched raw partner is T5-v1.1-base on the Latin corpus, since Sentence-T5 starts from an English T5 checkpoint rather than from LaTa.

The raw encoder-only cell is the discriminating test.
Both accounts predict that the raw T5s collapse and that LaBSE does not.
If LaBERTa and PhilBERTa keep retrieval at every depth, the collapse follows T5 pretraining.
If they collapse, it follows the missing embedding objective.
The embedding-trained T5 cell is weaker evidence, because its loss, like our fine-tuning below, attaches at the output.
A Sentence-T5 that collapses at mid-depth is then predicted by both accounts, while one that stays healthy favors the objective account.
We read this cell against LaBSE's healthy middle layers and against raw T5-v1.1-base.
An English model may also retrieve Latin poorly at every layer for reasons unrelated to collapse, so for this cell the label-free top-PC share and effective rank are the primary readout.

Fine-tuning gives a first piece of evidence.
Contrastive fine-tuning on 499 of the training pairs (Appendix~\ref{app:reference_systems}) raises LaTa's last layer from 0.938 to 0.984 AUROC (Table~\ref{tab:finetune_ceiling}).
Its layers 2 to 11 still collapse, at AUROC 0.50 to 0.57.
Their geometry barely moves: the peak top-PC share is 0.945 after fine-tuning against 0.952 before (Table~\ref{tab:ft_lata_layerwise}, Appendix~\ref{app:reference_systems}).
Supervision attached to the output thus leaves the intermediate layers unrepaired.
A similar output-only pattern is reported for a multilingual encoder: after sentence-level tuning it has no large outlier dimension at the output layer, while the base model's top outlier persists, reduced, at layer 8 \citep{haemmerl2023anisotropy}.
This result does not separate the two accounts: small-scale supervision at the output may leave intermediate layers untouched under either.
Our fine-tuning runs at most eight epochs on 499 pairs, far less than the large-scale contrastive training behind Sentence-T5, so the raw encoder-only cell remains the test.

All retrieval evidence comes from one labeled corpus.
The geometry, however, needs no labels, so we can cross model with text.
We compute top-PC share and effective rank per layer for mT5-base and PhilTa, whose pretraining covers English, on an English sample.
We compute the same for T5-v1.1-base \citep{raffel2020t5} on both the Latin corpus and the English sample.
If the collapse is a property of the model, each model shows the same profile on both texts: top-PC share above 0.8 and effective rank near 1 at mid-depth.
If mT5-base stays high-rank on English, the collapse depends on the input text.
If T5-v1.1-base stays high-rank on English while mT5-base collapses there, it depends on the pretraining data.
Every cell shows the same profile (Table~\ref{tab:gen_geometry} and Figure~\ref{fig:gen_geometry}, Appendix~\ref{app:layer_diagnostics}).
The English sample is 1,705 public-domain passages of US court opinions from the Caselaw Access Project \citep{cap2024}, as redistributed in the Common Pile \citep{kandpal2025commonpile}, one per Latin passage and matched to it in mT5 token length; as on Latin, we read the statistics on the 847 passages paired with training passages.

PhilTa's effective rank is 1.8 to 2.3 on both texts, as on Latin, rather than near 1.
T5-v1.1-base, pretrained on English alone, is low-rank from layer 2 to layer 11 on both texts, at top-PC share 0.93 to 0.95 on English and 0.96 to 0.97 on Latin.
In every cell the first and last layers stay high-rank.
Neither fail condition occurs: in these cells the low-rank middle layers appear on both texts, whether or not the input is in the model's pretraining language.
This check speaks to geometry only.
Whether retrieval also collapses on English text stays outside what this testbed measures.

\section{Repair and Its Limits}
\label{sec:repair}

Sections~\ref{sec:localizing} and~\ref{sec:objective} ask what masks the retrieval signal.
This section asks whether a train-only projection can remove the mask, and where it stops helping.
ABTT (Section~\ref{sec:postproc}) subtracts the training mean and removes the top $D$ training components; no model parameter changes.

\paragraph{ABTT restores every layer.}

Under SIF+ABTT every model places the correct directory in a two-item shortlist for more than 95 percent of queries (Table~\ref{tab:taskb}, Appendix~\ref{app:sif_variants}).
Directory-level bootstrap intervals (Appendix~\ref{app:ci_pq}, Table~\ref{tab:headline_ci}) span about $\pm 0.02$ AUROC and $\pm 3$ points of routing accuracy per cell.
Every zero-shot routing gain from ABTT excludes zero, as do the AUROC gains of the three T5 encoders and LaBSE; those of Qwen3-0.6B (0.966 to 0.973; 95\% CI of the gain $-0.010$ to $0.019$) and KaLM-mini (0.972 to 0.981; $-0.002$ to $0.021$) do not (Table~\ref{tab:headline_ci_diffs}).
The spread in DirAcc@1 falls from 39.3 (34.6 to 43.7) to 3.3 (1.8 to 6.0) points, and the lexical reference is not distinguishable from the best ABTT cells ($+0.5$; 95\% CI $-1.4$ to $2.5$ against Qwen3-0.6B, $-1.3$ to $2.3$ against KaLM-mini); only LaTa's ABTT cell is significantly below it.

\paragraph{The offset regime and the open-set decision.}
The embedding-trained models do not collapse, yet ABTT still changes their routing.
At their respective train-selected layers, their baseline AUROC was already 0.96 to 0.97.
Under ABTT, assignment accuracy rises from 83.3 to 90.8 for LaBSE, from 82.3 to 91.5 for Qwen3-0.6B, and from 87.5 to 91.7 for KaLM-mini.
ABTT also improves LaBSE's ranking, from 0.956 to 0.987 AUROC, while the AUROC gains of Qwen3-0.6B and KaLM-mini are within noise; DirAcc@1 gains 3.5 to 9.1 points across the three.
Centering alone, which removes the shared offset, does not account for this (below).

If an offset only added a constant to every cosine, order would be preserved and a threshold fit on training files would absorb it.
A shared offset vector need not act as such a constant.
Its effect on each cosine depends on the passage's norm and its projection onto the offset, so it can reorder pairs.
LaBSE's AUROC gain under ABTT does not come from the mean: centering alone leaves its AUROC unchanged ($-0.002$, $-0.009$ to $0.004$), and the gain arrives with the ten removed components ($+0.029$, $0.013$ to $0.048$).
A cost to the open-set decision beyond ranking would need offsets that differ by query, such as hub vectors that sit close to many queries \citep{radovanovic2010hubs}, or a shift between the training and test score distributions.
The threshold grid offers a mundane alternative.
We fit $\tau$ over 200 evenly spaced values in $[0,1]$, a step of about 0.005, while at layer 27 the test cosines of Qwen3-0.6B spread with a standard deviation of only 0.007.
At such a layer one grid step spans most of a standard deviation, and ABTT could help routing simply by spreading the cosines.
Five checks on cached embeddings test these readings (Table~\ref{tab:pq_routing}, Appendix~\ref{app:ci_pq}).
The gain does not depend on the threshold.
With no threshold at all, the AUROC of each file's best-match cosine for existing against new files rises from the baseline cell to the ABTT cell by 0.037 (95\% CI 0.021 to 0.055) for LaBSE, 0.055 (0.034 to 0.077) for Qwen3-0.6B, and 0.027 (0.012 to 0.043) for KaLM-mini.
At their baseline cells the train-fit $\tau$ gives assignment accuracy within 1.0 point of the best test threshold (upper bounds 1.1 to 3.5), and the gain survives when both settings use their best test thresholds (7.0, 8.9, and 4.4 points).
Refitting $\tau$ as the exact best-F1 cut leaves the three ABTT cells unchanged and moves the baselines by $-1.3$ to $+0.3$ points.
Layer 27 is not a routing cell: at Qwen3-0.6B's routing cells the test cosines spread with a standard deviation of 0.043 (layer 28, raw) and 0.086 after ABTT (layer 5; 0.034 raw).
Nor does the shared offset produce the gain.
At each baseline cell's layer, centering alone ($D=0$) changes assignment accuracy by $-4.7$ points ($-7.4$ to $-2.0$) for LaBSE, $+2.6$ ($-0.4$ to $5.5$) for Qwen3-0.6B, and $-1.9$ ($-3.7$ to $-0.1$) for KaLM-mini, and at the ABTT layer of each of the three the removed components add 12.0 to 25.9 points on top of centering.
From the baseline cell to the ABTT cell hubness also falls \citep{radovanovic2010hubs}: the skewness of the 10-occurrence distribution drops from 2.0--2.5 to 0.6--1.1, while centering alone at the ABTT layer leaves it near 1.8.
We therefore read the routing gain as better separation of known from new witnesses by their best match, carried by the top components beyond the mean; whether the reduced hubness causes it remains untested.

\paragraph{SIF.}
SIF never matches ABTT on any model or task; we use it only as the diagnostic of Section~\ref{sec:geometry} (full sweeps in Appendix~\ref{app:sif_variants}).

\paragraph{Where the repair stops helping.}
ABTT does not help fine-tuned ranking: AUROC falls by 0.002 to 0.014 in point estimate, and every 95\% interval includes zero.
Fine-tuned LaTa falls from 0.984 to 0.970 ($-0.014$, $-0.032$ to $+0.001$), and Qwen3-0.6B and KaLM-mini fall by 0.002 and 0.003 ($-0.005$ to $+0.001$; $-0.007$ to $+0.001$); for Qwen3-0.6B the two rows also sit at different train-selected layers (28 and 27).
The same projection raises fine-tuned LaTa's DirAcc@1 from 81.6 to 85.2 ($+3.6$, 1.0 to 6.3); the rises for Qwen3-0.6B (90.8 to 91.4) and KaLM-mini (91.7 to 92.5) are within noise (Table~\ref{tab:finetune_ceiling}).
The direction of the ranking loss is consistent with \citet{rajaee2021finetuning}, who found that removing top directions lowers the STS performance of BERT after Siamese fine-tuning.
\citet{reichbauer2026alignment} find, by contrast, that whitening still helps fine-tuned Latin and Greek encoders.
Whether the projection costs ranking in fine-tuned models is the subspace question of Section~\ref{sec:localizing}: if fine-tuning moves task signal into the top components, then removing them removes signal (E3).
E3 finds that fine-tuning does move signal there: at layer 12 the removed subspace of fine-tuned LaTa ranks at 0.953, against 0.868 before fine-tuning and 0.900 for the next ten components.

Fine-tuning on 499 training pairs also beats the training-free projection for the embedding-trained models.
Over five random reassignments of query and reference files, fine-tuned Qwen3-0.6B with ABTT routes at 92.3 against 90.5 for its zero-shot ABTT row, and KaLM-mini at 93.0 against 90.9; for LaTa the two tie at 87.7 (Appendix~\ref{app:reference_systems}).
On the single split the DirAcc@1 differences are $+2.0$ (0.0 to 4.0) and $+3.1$ (1.2 to 5.2).

\paragraph{Practitioner rule.}
On this testbed, a top-$D$ projection fit on training vectors helped every pre-trained encoder at every layer and did not help ranking in contrastively fine-tuned ones.
A high top-PC share (0.76 or more here) flags a collapsed layer; mean pairwise cosine did not.
Neither statistic chooses among healthy layers, and a character n-gram reference routes as well as the best pre-trained configuration on this corpus.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The analysis describes one failure precisely.
In the middle layers of the T5 encoders in our panel, a low-dimensional subspace takes over the pooled vectors, and cosine ranking falls to or near chance.
One direction holds most of its variance, but removing it alone restores only part of the ranking; removing two to five restores nearly all of it.
Top-PC share separates these layers from healthy ones; mean pairwise cosine does not.
In the pre-trained models the removed directions rank below the retained ones, and at the selected layers below the next ten as well; in the fine-tuned models they rank above the next ten, a reversal for LaTa (E3).
\pending{E1, E2: whether massive coordinates explain the subspace}.
A projection fit on training vectors removes these few directions and restores ranking at every depth.
Choosing among healthy layers still needs a train-only task signal.

Several observations remain open.
Qwen3-0.6B and KaLM-mini are decoders of the kind in which massive activations are reported \citep{sun2024massive}, yet they never collapse; the E2 audit tests whether their coordinates shift passages together instead of varying across them.
At mT5-base layer 1, a few massive coordinates carry much of the pooled cosine, yet the layer still ranks at AUROC 0.82.
In LaTa's middle layers the raw cosine gap is negative ($-0.05$ to $-0.07$), so same-directory pairs score slightly below different-directory pairs on average, while AUROC stays at chance.
Section~\ref{sec:geometry} shows that the standardized gap does not account for it, and the token audit (E2) tests a length account.
Architecture and training objective are confounded in the models we have run, so the T5 attribution stays provisional until the controlled panel of Section~\ref{sec:objective} lands.
% CONDITIONAL: cite mikkelsen2026 as contrast if the 2x2 panel supports a T5-specific account

For the editor who needs to route new witnesses, the repair narrows the choice of model (Section~\ref{sec:repair}).
Under SIF+ABTT every model places the correct directory in a two-item shortlist for more than 95 percent of queries.
The existing-versus-new call remains wrong for roughly one file in ten, so the open-set decision stays with the scholar.
A character n-gram reference routes as well as any repaired pre-trained encoder on this corpus, and a practical tool should start from it.
We release the frozen testbed and split, the evaluation code, and lexical reference scores so that others can test these accounts on their own encoders.
Token attributions at the train-selected layers are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:attribution}; ABTT yields leave-one-out rank faithfulness gains there, with mixed ERASER-style rationale metrics.

\section*{Limitations}

The study uses one labeled corpus in one language and one domain: medieval Latin canon law. A label-free check extends the geometry, but not the retrieval result, to one English register (Section~\ref{sec:objective}). The 1,705-witness corpus is large for this field but small by current NLP standards. We test six models, and others may behave differently. In the models we have run, architecture and training objective are confounded, so we cannot yet say whether the mid-depth collapse follows from T5 pretraining or from the absence of an embedding objective; the controlled panel of Section~\ref{sec:objective} is designed to separate them (P2x2). The bootstrap intervals resample test directories with the layer, threshold, and projections held fixed, so they cover test-set sampling but not the choice of split; they span about $\pm 0.02$ AUROC and $\pm 3$ routing points per cell, and differences whose paired interval includes zero, among them the ABTT drops on the fine-tuned encoders and the lexical reference's lead over the best ABTT cells, are not established effects. The printed routing accuracies are precise to a few files: refitting the threshold on a finer grid moves 45 of the 66 printed routing numbers, by a median of 3 files (0.35 points); ABTT, SIF+ABTT, and fine-tuned cells move by at most 0.8, and Base and SIF cells by up to 2.6 (Appendix~\ref{app:ci_pq}). ABTT needs enough text to estimate principal components, so smaller archives need separate study.

Task~B measures ranking quality and a single threshold decision. It does not measure the full judgment a working scholar brings to deciding whether a witness belongs to an existing directory. The attribution analysis in the appendix is local to fixed query-candidate scores and failed its pre-registered threshold; its consistent effect is a leave-one-out rank faithfulness gain, and the ERASER-style rationale metrics are mixed.

The labels are expert-assigned, but by one project. Its director or supervised graduate students assigned the keys, and verification is by collation in the project's parallel display, not by independent double annotation, so no inter-annotator agreement figure exists for this testbed. Collation compares the witnesses of one key, so it has nothing to compare when a key has a single witness, and 545 of the 840 directories hold one witness in this testbed.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}

The benchmark holds early medieval legal texts, transcribed from manuscripts and early printings, and contains no personal data. The Carolingian Canon Law project publishes its material under a Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial-ShareAlike 4.0 licence \citep{ccl2026about}. The project has confirmed in writing that the licence covers the transcriptions, annotations and markup in the exports we use and that, in the view of the project's Advisory Board, attribution to the project suffices; the project's interface credits the transcriber and proofreader of each unit. The project's corpus keeps growing and being corrected, so benchmark version 1 is a dated snapshot, fixed by a sha256 manifest of its 1,705 labelled files, and every number in this paper comes from it. We will release it under the same licence, in coordination with the project's periodic versioned exports, and later versions will follow those exports. The intended use is research on retrieval and on assisted source identification: the system ranks candidates for an expert to judge and does not identify sources on its own.

\section{Corpus Provenance and Derivation}
\label{app:corpus_provenance}

This appendix records where the testbed text comes from and how our files were derived from it, in the detail that Section~\ref{sec:dataset} leaves out.

\paragraph{The project.} The Carolingian Canon Law project \citep[CCL;][]{firey2009ccl} is an open-access digital edition founded in 2009; \citet{eichbauer2014ccl} reviews it.\footnote{Founding year, pre-2019 workflow, proofreading standard, the one manuscript transcribed by handwritten text recognition (HTR), the design of the identifiers, the assignment and verification of keys, and the extent of unidentified material: unpublished documentation and correspondence supplied by the project, 2026.} Its texts are early medieval canon-law manuscripts, most of them ninth-century and never edited to modern standards. Each manuscript is identified by its siglum (Place-Library-Shelfmark), and a witness is one manuscript's copy of one unit (one transcribed canon or excerpt).

\paragraph{Transcription.} Transcription is manual, from manuscript images or, for some texts, eighteenth-century printings, with one exception: HTR has been attempted on one manuscript (Paris, BnF, lat.\ 1454; 142 of the 1,705 witnesses), and its transcription is still in proofreading. Before 2019 transcribers worked from purchased microfilm into word-processed files, which CCL contributors converted to TEI-P5 (the Text Encoding Initiative's XML standard for scholarly editions). Since 2019 they transcribe in the CCL Transcription Desk beside a IIIF image of the folio (a zoomable web-served scan of the manuscript leaf); an approved proofreader on the Proofreading Desk then checks the text against the image to the letter and adds TEI markup \citep{ccl2026desks}. That proofreading stage belongs to the post-2019 workflow. The interface credits the transcriber and proofreader of each unit \citep{ccl2026about}.

\paragraph{Identifiers.} The project has two identifier systems, both designed at its launch in 2009 by its director in consultation with a mathematician, a software developer and a second scholar of early medieval canon law. The first is a location identifier. Every manuscript is a unique object, so every unit has exactly one place in one manuscript, and the identifier records it as the manuscript siglum, the folium, and the unit's position on that folium. Our file names are these identifiers. The second is the source key. Many units in different manuscripts copy the same prescription issued by the same authority, and the key names that common source. It encodes the type of source (a church council, a papal decretal, a royal capitulary, a theologian, a penitential), the point of origin (a particular council, pope or author), the date of origin, and the item's reference in modern scholarship, such as a canon, letter or register number. The key is designed so that a researcher can find the modern authoritative edition of the source where one exists.

\paragraph{Labels.} CCL keys a unit to its original source, such as a fourth-century council or a papal decretal, once that source is identified, and our directories reuse that key as supplied. In a label, a four-letter code carries the type of source and its point of origin, followed by the year and the canon or chapter number, as in \texttt{CTOU.567.16}; some labels carry a trailing annotation, as in \texttt{CTOU.567.16 (15)} or \texttt{CARL.501?.18}. Counting a directory as key-named when its label begins with a four-letter code, a period, a year and a period, whatever follows, 689 of the 840 directory labels are source keys. The other 151 are modern edition citations, the Apostolic Canons (\texttt{Can.apost.}$N$), references to other collections such as the Capitula Martini, biblical references, and a handful of unidentified sources; each unidentified source is a singleton directory and contributes no positive pairs.

\paragraph{Assignment and verification.} Keys were assigned by the project's director, a specialist in early medieval canon law, or by graduate students working under the director's supervision, who had enough Latin to check a manuscript version against the modern authoritative version of the source. Verification, and occasional correction, runs through the project's parallel display, which sets side by side all units that share a key and shows how far their wording agrees. A misidentified unit stands out there as a text that cannot be a version of the others. This is verification by collation (see Limitations). The project supplied two label corrections during this study, and both are logged with the frozen testbed. We assigned no label ourselves and changed only those two. What remains unidentified is large: across the several hundred manuscripts that contain the project's texts, thousands of units still carry no key, and they are the queries that Task B is meant to serve.

\paragraph{Derivation.} We hold the witnesses as plain text derived from CCL TEI-P5 exports. The rule is one file per unit: the directory is the source key from the unit's \texttt{scholSource} division, which is stripped from the text so that no file carries its own label; the text is the manuscript rubric followed by the canon text, with inline markup flattened (editorial notes kept, deletions dropped) and layout whitespace collapsed; a unit without a source key receives no directory. The rule is scripted; checked against a current export of one manuscript (Paris, BnF, lat.\ 2123), it reproduces all 279 units present in both the export and the corpus, 100 percent after whitespace normalization with no character-level disagreement, and places every one of them in the same directory.

\paragraph{Why whitening is excluded.} Full-rank PCA whitening \citep{su2021whitening} leaves the different-directory cosine mean at or below 0.015, so the learned threshold degenerates: across all 100 whitening cells every file is routed to an existing directory, existing accuracy is 1.000, new accuracy never exceeds one file in 323, and assignment accuracy sits at the 62.4 percent class prior. That prior beats baseline in 60 of 100 cells while DirAcc@1 never exceeds 41.5 percent, so the apparent gain is a thresholding artifact. SIF and full-rank whitening fall short of ABTT for different reasons. SIF acts before pooling, so it can only attenuate the frequent tokens that feed the dominant direction, and whatever survives that reweighting is still pooled into the passage vector. Full-rank whitening also rescales the near-null directions that 847 training vectors cannot estimate (condition numbers $3\times10^{5}$ to $10^{16}$), which amplifies noise and degenerates the routing threshold; reduced-rank whitening drops them and does not degenerate it (Table~\ref{tab:whiten_reduced}).

\paragraph{Top-$k$ ranking.} In the top-$k$ ranking form of Task B, each directory receives its best document match score, a pseudo-option \texttt{NEW} receives score $\tau$, and the simulated expert succeeds when the labeled directory appears in the shortlist. Cumulative top-$K$ accuracy is the fraction of test queries whose labeled directory is among the top $K$ options. Table~\ref{tab:taskb} gives the shortlist view (per-layer: Appendix~\ref{app:per_layer_headline}): every model exceeds 95\% by Top-2 and gains little after Top-3.

Three encoders are fine-tuned under one recipe, LaTa, Qwen3-0.6B and KaLM-mini. Fine-tuning only the Latin-pretrained model would leave the ceiling confounded with Latin pretraining, so the second model is a multilingual decoder-only encoder with no Latin-specific pretraining, Qwen3-0.6B. The third answers the remaining question, whether supervision can clear the best cell the correction produces anywhere in the paper: KaLM-mini holds it, at 91.7 assignment accuracy and at 89.4 DirAcc@1 tied with Qwen3-0.6B. All three are trained with symmetric InfoNCE over positive pairs and in-batch negatives (no two pairs from one directory in a batch), temperature 0.05, AdamW at learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$ with weight decay 0.01, linear warmup over the first 10 percent of steps then linear decay, gradient clipping at 1.0, 16 pairs per batch, up to 8 epochs with early stopping after 3 epochs without dev improvement, and seed 42. All three are mean-pooled with the same token filter as their zero-shot rows, so the fine-tuned vectors live in the space those rows are measured in; Qwen3-0.6B and KaLM-mini add gradient checkpointing, which recomputes activations rather than storing them and leaves the gradients unchanged. The dev slice holds out 28 of the 190 train directories with at least two files (71 files), the same directories for every model, so each trains on 499 of the 565 positive train pairs, and the checkpoint is chosen by dev DirAcc@1 with dev AUROC as the tie-break. Accuracy differences at or below one dev file are treated as ties, because one file out of 71 is the pool's whole resolution; without that, a model whose pre-trained encoder already routes all 71 dev files can never select a trained checkpoint. A 71-file pool is the weak link in this protocol: one borderline file flipped between two otherwise identical Qwen3-0.6B runs at a training loss of $6\times10^{-4}$, which is GPU non-determinism rather than a difference in the model, and it is worth 1.4 points of the selection criterion. LaTa selects epoch 7, the last epoch run before early stopping fired, and its ABTT sweep selects $D=10$, the top of the grid, at every layer, so the tuned and fixed ABTT variants coincide there. Qwen3-0.6B selects epoch 3 of 6 run, and its sweep is a genuine one, selecting the top of the grid at 12 of 28 layers and $D=2$ at the layer its Task B row reports. KaLM-mini selects epoch 1 of 4 run, the only model of the three whose pre-trained encoder leaves the dev pool room to improve, and its sweep selects the top of the grid at 19 of 24 layers including the layer its Task B row reports. All three rows are ceilings at this training budget rather than asymptotes: training loss reaches $4\times10^{-5}$ on Qwen3-0.6B by epoch 4 and $3\times10^{-5}$ on KaLM-mini, so the 499 pairs are memorized and more epochs buy nothing.

\section{Bootstrap Intervals and Routing Checks}

\paragraph{Intervals.} A bootstrap replicate draws the 514 test directories with replacement ($B=10{,}000$, seed 233). A drawn directory keeps every file and pair: a directory drawn $c$ times weights its files and within-directory pairs by $c$ and its pairs with a directory drawn $c'$ times by $cc'$, and no pair is formed between two copies of one directory. The layer, threshold, ABTT components, $D$, and SIF weights stay fit on the training split, and every score is computed once on the full test set, so a replicate only reweights test pairs and files. Intervals are 95\% percentiles; a paired difference recomputes both cells on each replicate. Before resampling, every cell was recomputed with the paper's evaluation code and matched its printed value. \section{Token Attribution at Retrieval-Selected Layers}
\label{app:attribution}

This appendix compares local explanations of the retrieval score before and after ABTT: methods, metrics, setup, and results. Appendix~\ref{app:attribution_sweeps} gives the secondary metrics and sweeps, and Appendix~\ref{app:qual_attribution} gives one qualitative example.

\subsection{Local Attribution Methods}

We evaluate local explanations for a fixed query-candidate retrieval score.
The design goal is a review aid: attributions are shown as highlights connecting matching tokens across the two witnesses, so a scholar judging a proposed match can see which words the score rests on.
Our metrics test faithfulness, whether a highlight tracks what the score depends on, not plausibility, whether it points at the words a philologist would compare \citep{jacovi2020faithfully}.
Integrated gradients \citep{sundararajan2017axiomatic} attributes the pairwise cosine to the input tokens of each side of the pair (pair-matrix view: Figure~\ref{fig:pairmatrix}).

Our learned-mask attribution is adapted from MaRC \citep{brinnerzarriess2023marc}. MaRC was proposed for classification, where a soft input mask preserves a target class score while sparsity and smoothness regularizers encourage concise rationales. We keep the per-instance mask optimization and replace the classification target with pairwise retrieval cosine: the mask is optimized on one side of a query-candidate pair while the partner representation stays fixed, and swapping the sides gives candidate-side attribution.

Let $\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}_q) \in \mathbb{R}^{T_q \times d_{\text{emb}}}$ denote the input-embedding lookup, $E_{>0}$ the encoder with the lookup removed, and $\mathbf{z}_c^{\star}$ the candidate representation held fixed during optimization. We parameterize $\mathbf{m} = \sigma(\boldsymbol{\zeta})$ with free logits $\boldsymbol{\zeta} \in \mathbb{R}^{T_q}$ and write $\mathcal{I}$ for content-token positions. For the ABTT variant, define $s_{\mathrm{ABTT}}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})=\cos(\mathrm{ABTT}(\mathbf{a}),\mathbf{b})$. The retrieval-adapted MaRC loss is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{m}) =
&\bigl(s_{\mathrm{ABTT}}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_q(\mathbf{m}), \mathbf{z}_c^{\star})
- s_{\mathrm{ABTT}}(\mathbf{z}_q, \mathbf{z}_c^{\star})\bigr)^2 \\
&+ \lambda \sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}} \frac{m_i}{|\mathcal{I}|}
+ \gamma \sum_{i,i+1\in\mathcal{I}} \frac{|m_{i+1}-m_i|}{|\mathcal{I}|-1},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:retrieval_mark}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_q(\mathbf{m}) = \mathrm{pool}(E_{>0}(\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{m})\,\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}_q)), \mathbf{a}_q)$. The baseline variant uses raw cosine in place of the ABTT-corrected cosine in Equation~\ref{eq:retrieval_mark}. Logits outside $\mathcal{I}$ are frozen at $-\infty$.

\subsection{Attribution Metrics}

We report two threshold-free metrics: $\rho_{\mathrm{LOO}}$, the Spearman correlation between attribution magnitude and the leave-one-out drop in the same retrieval score, and the DelAUC gap, the random-order minus attribution-order deletion-curve area. A shuffled-attribution control recomputes each of six candidate metrics ($\rho_{\mathrm{LOO}}$, Kendall $\tau_b$, the DelAUC and InsAUC gaps, AOPC comprehensiveness and sufficiency) on five permutations of the attribution's own scores in all twelve model-method-variant cells; a metric qualifies for the headline table (Table~\ref{tab:attribution_metrics_main}) only if the real attribution beats the permutation mean in every cell at the operational layers. ERASER-style sufficiency and comprehensiveness \citep{deyoung2020eraser} and MinFrac, defined for retrieval cosine in Appendix~\ref{app:attribution_sweeps}, stay out of the headline table because their verdicts depend on the threshold and on the erasure operator. Every curve metric ranks tokens by $|a|$, ties broken by position; Appendix~\ref{app:attribution_sweeps} reports them and states both conventions and their consequences.

\subsection{Setup and Layer Selection}

MaRC runs up to 200 Adam steps per pair (learning rate 0.1, stopping after step 50 once the masked cosine is within 0.01 of the full one) from $\boldsymbol{\zeta}_0 = \log 9$ with $\lambda = 0.01$ and $\gamma = 0.001$; integrated gradients uses 40 integration steps. Attribution layers follow a predeclared train-only rule, a variant of the Task~B rule of Section~\ref{sec:layers}: the earliest layer within 0.5 points of the best training-set DirAcc@1 under ABTT. It selects LaTa layer 7, PhilTa layer 1, and mT5-base layer 1 for attribution, with $D=10$ ABTT components; the most anisotropic layers on the test split, LaTa 8, PhilTa 6, and mT5-base 5, are kept as mechanism checks (Appendix~\ref{app:layer_diagnostics}). The rule locks these layers before any attribution metric is computed.

\section{Secondary Attribution Metrics and Sweeps}
\label{app:attribution_sweeps}

\paragraph{Ranking conventions.} Two ranking conventions hold for every curve metric and every method, variant and control alike. Tokens are ranked by attribution magnitude $|a|$ rather than by signed importance, because the question is which tokens carry the pair cosine and both methods produce two-signed scores. Ties are broken by token position, which matters most for MaRC, whose many exactly-zero scores are then ordered by reading order. Absolute values are therefore not comparable with a signed-ranking implementation, and Kendall $\tau_b$ in Table~\ref{tab:attribution_metrics_secondary} is the tie-corrected companion to $\rho_{\mathrm{LOO}}$ under the second convention.

\paragraph{Secondary metrics.} Figure~\ref{fig:attribution_rho_loo_main} plots the rank-faithfulness column of Table~\ref{tab:attribution_metrics_main}. Table~\ref{tab:attribution_metrics_secondary} carries the metrics Table~\ref{tab:attribution_metrics_main} does not: tie-corrected Kendall $\tau_b$, chance-corrected insertion faithfulness, and the three ERASER-style rationale metrics at the global threshold settings. Sufficiency keeps the top-ranked query tokens and measures how much of the full pair cosine remains, comprehensiveness removes those tokens and measures the score drop, and MinFrac reports the smallest retained query-token fraction needed to recover a fixed share of the full cosine. Four computed quantities are printed in neither table: the raw deletion and insertion areas, which appear here only as the chance-corrected gaps, and AOPC-Sufficiency and AOPC-Comprehensiveness, whose per-threshold components are in the sweeps below. Table~\ref{tab:attribution_shuffle_control} gives the per-cell shuffled-attribution gaps for the six candidate metrics, the control Appendix~\ref{app:attribution} quotes as a validity statement. The rest of this appendix expands those rationale metrics across Sufficiency and Comprehensiveness thresholds of 10\%, 25\%, and 50\%, and MinFrac recovery thresholds of 0.70, 0.80, 0.90, and 0.95. We use one global midpoint setting rather than selecting thresholds per model or per method. Table~\ref{tab:attribution_sweep_main_methods} gives the sweep for integrated gradients and retrieval-adapted MaRC, and Table~\ref{tab:attribution_sweep_supplemental_methods} gives it for the supplemental attribution methods.
