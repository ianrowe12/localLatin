1. Where the attribution experiment fits in the paper

The attribution experiment is a supporting plank, not the headline. The paper's main claim is about retrieval: a layerwise diagnosis of mid-layer collapse in Latin-adapted T5 encoders, plus a train-free geometric repair (ABTT) that restores semantic separability. Attribution enters only as the third contribution, which argues that the same correction also yields better model explanations via integrated gradients.

Contribution (3), verbatim:

“a simple train-free geometric correction restores semantic separability across layers and models, and that the corrected representations yield sharper, more localized integrated-gradient attributions, supporting an inspectable semantic matching pipeline for humanities scholars.”

Two clauses, two different jobs. The first clause (“restores semantic separability”) is carried by Tasks A and B, which are retrieval experiments measured by AUROC and Top-1 accuracy. The second clause (“sharper, more localized integrated-gradient attributions”) is carried by the attribution experiment we are scrutinizing this week. Only the second clause depends on the IG numbers; the first stands or falls on retrieval alone.

The retrieval headline is already in hand. On Task A (pairwise duplicate detection), ABTT lifts mid-layer AUROC from near chance to above 0.97 on every Latin-adapted and multilingual T5 we tested:

On Task B (directory routing, 5-seed averaged Top-1), all six models converge in a tight band near 90 percent: LaTa 90.1, PhilTa 90.0, LaBSE 90.4, mT5-base 89.8, Qwen3-0.6B 90.3, KaLM-mini 89.4. These results were computed without integrated gradients and would survive intact if the attribution experiment were removed.

Key takeaway. If the attribution numbers are weaker than we hoped, the paper's headline contribution is unaffected. What is at stake is the second clause of contribution (3), the claim that ABTT also produces better explanations, which is a value-add to the inspectable-pipeline story rather than its foundation.

The rest of this document asks a single question: does the supporting plank actually carry the load contribution (3) assigns to it, and if not, what claim is the current evidence strong enough to support?

2. MaRC, and how we adapted it for retrieval

MaRC (Brinner and Zarriess, 2023) was introduced as a model-agnostic interpretability method for classifiers. Given an input sequence and a trained model that emits a class logit, MaRC learns a per-input soft mask over the input tokens, optimized so that masking the input through the mask preserves the model's predicted logit while simultaneously driving most mask values toward zero. The output is a continuous attribution score over input positions: tokens whose mask values stay near one are the ones the classifier needed in order to keep predicting the same class, and tokens whose mask values collapse to near zero are evidence that the model could discard them without changing its decision. The appeal of MaRC over post-hoc gradient methods is that it explicitly searches for a minimal sufficient subset rather than reading off a local linearization.

Our retrieval setting has no class logit. What we care about is whether two manuscript fragments embed near each other under a given layer and post-processing variant. We therefore replace the classification target with a cosine similarity target. Concretely, for a pair (query q, candidate c) we compute the partner embedding p = pool(forward(c, layer=L)) once, register it as a buffer with no gradient, and treat it as a fixed reference vector. The MaRC objective then asks: which subset of tokens in q is sufficient to reproduce the cosine similarity between the unmasked q pool and p? A classification logit lives in a discrete output space, but cosine similarity is a continuous scalar between two vectors and can be pulled in many directions by ablating different tokens, which makes the search non-trivial. Gradient descent does not care about that distinction so long as the partner is held fixed, which is exactly what the buffer registration enforces.

Key insight. MaRC is normally used to explain a classifier's predicted class. We retarget it at a continuous similarity score against a fixed partner embedding, turning it into a saliency method for retrieval pairs. Tokens whose mask survives optimization are the ones responsible for the pair's cosine, at the chosen layer and variant.

The mechanics are straightforward. We parameterize a soft mask m = sigmoid(zeta) with one logit per query token position, initialized so that m starts near one everywhere (full unmasking). At each optimization step we embed the query tokens, multiply the embedding tensor by m elementwise across positions, contextualize through the encoder up to layer L, mean-pool, optionally apply ABTT cleaning using pre-fit PCs and mean vector, and compute cosine against the fixed p. The loss combines a fidelity term, a sparsity term, and a smoothness term:

$$L(m) = (\cos_m - \cos_{\text{orig}})^2 + \lambda \, \overline{m_{\text{content}}} + \gamma \, \overline{|m_{i+1} - m_i|}$$

where cos_orig is the cosine of the fully unmasked query against the partner, computed once and frozen as the target; λ = 0.01 pushes mask values for content positions toward zero so the explanation is compact; and γ = 0.001 penalizes sharp on/off boundaries between adjacent positions, encouraging contiguous spans rather than scattered single tokens. We run 200 steps of Adam at lr=0.1, with early stopping when the relative cosine gap |cos_m - cos_orig| / |cos_orig| drops below 0.05 and the mask sparsity has converged. The converged m* is the MaRC attribution for that pair, layer, and variant.

Each labeled pair is run through MaRC four times: query attribution under the baseline (raw mean-pool) cosine target, query attribution under the ABTT cosine target, and the same two for candidate attribution by swapping the roles of q and c. The four invocations are mirrored exactly on the IG side, which is what makes the head-to-head comparison in §4 a clean ablation rather than an apples-to-oranges contrast. Section 3 lays out the matching IG invocations.

One honest caveat before we move on. The sparsity coefficient λ = 0.01 is not neutral. By construction, MaRC actively pushes mask mass toward zero, which means converged masks tend to be sparser than what IG produces from a pure gradient integral with no compactness prior. This will matter when we look at Compactness@0.8 in §4 and §7: a method that bakes sparsity into its objective is, unsurprisingly, going to score well on a sparsity-flavored metric. We flag this here so the comparison with IG is read with the right priors rather than treated as a free win for MaRC.

3. What IG is, and why we don't “adapt” it

Integrated Gradients is scalar-agnostic. Sundararajan et al. (2017) introduce IG over class logits because that is the application they care about, but the definition only requires a differentiable scalar function of the input. A class logit is one such scalar. A cosine similarity against a fixed partner is another. We are not adapting IG to retrieval, we are substituting a different scalar into the same machinery. Nothing in the line integral, the completeness axiom, or the implementation changes.

Concretely, for a model $F$ with input tokens $x = (x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ and an embedding layer $E$ producing $E(x_i) \in \mathbb{R}^d$, the Layer Integrated Gradients attribution to position $i$ is

$$\text{IG}_i(x) \;=\; \bigl(E(x_i) - E(x'_i)\bigr) \cdot \int_0^1 \frac{\partial F\bigl(E(x') + \alpha\,(E(x) - E(x'))\bigr)}{\partial E_i}\, d\alpha,$$

where $x'$ is the baseline. We take the standard Captum LIG choice of a zero baseline at the embedding layer, $E(x'_i) = \mathbf{0}$ for all $i$, so the prefactor collapses to $E(x_i)$ and the integrand interpolates from the zero embedding to the real one. Captum approximates the integral by a Riemann sum at $n_{\text{steps}} = 40$,

$$\text{IG}_i(x) \;\approx\; E(x_i) \cdot \frac{1}{40} \sum_{k=1}^{40} \frac{\partial F(\alpha_k\, E(x))}{\partial E_i}, \qquad \alpha_k = k/40.$$

At each step the model is run forward with scaled embeddings and the gradient $\partial F / \partial E_i$ is collected. Captum returns a tensor of shape $(\text{seq\_len}, d)$. We collapse the hidden axis by summing, $\text{score}_i = \sum_{j=1}^{d} \text{IG}_{i,j}$, so each token gets a single scalar attribution comparable across positions.

For one pair $(q, c)$ at layer $L$ we instantiate four targets, each a Captum Module whose forward(input_ids, attention_mask) returns a single scalar cosine. We then build LayerIntegratedGradients(target, model.embedding_layer) and call .attribute(input_ids, additional_forward_args=(attention_mask,), n_steps=40, internal_batch_size=1). The four targets are: (i) query_base_target, $\cos(\text{pool}(F(q)_L), \text{pool}(F(c)_L))$ attributed to $q$; (ii) query_abtt_target, the same with ABTT applied to both pooled vectors, attributed to $q$; (iii) candidate_base_target, attributing to $c$ against raw pooled $q$; (iv) candidate_abtt_target, attributing to $c$ against ABTT-cleaned pooled $q$. The partner side is held fixed and gradients flow only through the attributed side, exactly mirroring the asymmetric structure in §2's MaRC formulation. The professor will recognize the parallel: in both cases one side is the probe, the other is a frozen anchor, and we measure how the probe's tokens push the scalar.

The zero baseline deserves a sentence. The standard worry with IG is that baseline choice can move attributions substantially, and for text the common alternatives are an all-PAD sequence or all-zeros at the embedding layer. We use all-zeros because it is the most neutral and the default LIG choice in Captum, ABTT effects are visible under it without ambiguity, and our claims in §7 are about relative orderings between cosine variants on the same tokens, not absolute attribution magnitudes. A PAD baseline gave qualitatively identical orderings in spot checks and was not pursued.

To frame this once more, plainly: IG is $(\text{input} - \text{baseline}) \cdot \int \text{grad}$. Nothing in that form is specific to classification, and calling the cosine substitution an “extension” or an “adaptation” overstates what is happening. The target function is different code, the math is unchanged. The IG numbers reported in §7 are produced by these four calls per pair, on 200 positive pairs per model.

4. The four metrics, and where each formula actually came from

This section defines the four faithfulness metrics used in the 200-positive-pair attribution evaluation, with the exact formulas as implemented in src/attribution_metrics.py and the correct citation for each.

Correction to Friday's meeting. Ian said “all formulas come from the MaRC paper.” That is incorrect. Sufficiency@k%, Comprehensiveness@k%, and Compactness@$\tau$ are from ERASER (DeYoung et al. 2020, ACL); MaRC uses them but did not invent them. $\rho_{\text{LOO}}$ is closer to Atanasova et al. 2020 (EMNLP), which evaluates faithfulness via Spearman correlation between attribution magnitude and per-token leave-one-out probability drop. We adapted it to cosine retrieval: per-token LOO cosine drop in place of class-probability drop. Provenance matters here because reviewers will check it.

Setup. Let $q$ be the query token sequence of length $n$, $c$ the partner candidate, and $S(\cdot, c) = \cos(\text{pool}(\text{model}(\cdot)[L]), \text{pool}(\text{model}(c)[L]))$ the layer-$L$ cosine (with optional ABTT applied to both sides). Let $a_i$ be the attribution score for token $i$, and let $S_{\text{full}} = S(q, c)$ be the unmasked cosine.

4.1 Sufficiency@k% (ERASER)

$$\text{Suff}@k\%_{\text{raw}} = S(q_{\text{top-}k\%}, c)$$ $$\text{Suff}@k\%_{\text{ratio}} = \frac{S(q_{\text{top-}k\%}, c)}{S_{\text{full}}}$$

Here $q_{\text{top-}k\%}$ keeps the top $\lceil k\% \cdot n \rceil$ tokens by $|a_i|$, replacing the rest with PAD and zeroing their attention. Reported at $k \in \{10\%, 25\%, 50\%\}$. The ratio is gated to NaN when $|S_{\text{full}}| < 0.05$ to avoid division blow-up; tables use the ratio. Source: ERASER (DeYoung et al. 2020). Implementation: attribution_metrics.py:182-218. Higher is better (kept tokens preserve cosine).

4.2 Comprehensiveness@k% (ERASER)

$$\text{Comp}@k\%_{\text{drop}} = S_{\text{full}} - S(q_{\text{complement-}k\%}, c)$$ $$\text{Comp}@k\%_{\text{ratio}} = \frac{S_{\text{full}} - S(q_{\text{complement-}k\%}, c)}{S_{\text{full}}}$$

Here $q_{\text{complement-}k\%}$ removes the top-$k\%$ tokens and keeps only the bottom $1-k\%$. The drop should be large if the top tokens were genuinely doing the work. Source: ERASER (DeYoung et al. 2020). Implementation: attribution_metrics.py:221-257. Higher is better (removing top tokens drops cosine more).

4.3 Compactness@$\tau$ (ERASER family)

$$\text{Compactness}@\tau = \min \left\{ \frac{k}{n} \;\Big|\; \frac{S(q_{\text{top-}k}, c)}{S_{\text{full}}} \ge \tau \right\}$$

The smallest fraction of tokens needed to recover at least $\tau$ of the full cosine, with $\tau = 0.80$ in our tables. This is a “how far down the ranking” metric rather than an endpoint metric. Source: ERASER-family rationale-length measure (DeYoung et al. 2020). Implementation: attribution_metrics.py:260-282. Lower is better (fewer tokens needed).

4.4 $\rho_{\text{LOO}}$ (adapted from Atanasova et al. 2020)

For each token $i$, compute the leave-one-out cosine drop

$$\delta_i = S_{\text{full}} - S(q_{\setminus i}, c)$$

and then

$$\rho_{\text{LOO}} = \mathrm{spearmanr}(|a|, \delta).$$

Tokens with $|\delta_i| < 10^{-6}$ are filtered as NaN, and at least 3 usable tokens are required to report a value. Source: Atanasova et al. 2020 (EMNLP), adapted from class-probability drops to per-token cosine drops. Implementation: attribution_metrics.py:285-321. Higher is better (the attribution ranking matches what actually happens under token removal).

Sense-check. Sufficiency and Comprehensiveness are endpoint metrics (does keeping or removing the top tokens preserve or destroy the cosine?). Compactness is a ranking-depth metric. $\rho_{\text{LOO}}$ is a whole-ranking faithfulness metric. The four together cover the standard space a reviewer will ask about.

5. Why negatives don't fit ERASER, and why we moved to positives only

ERASER-style faithfulness metrics presume the model's signal is a positive quantity to be localized; on negative pairs that premise breaks, and the metric ends up rewarding noise.

The trivially-win-by-masking pathology. A negative pair “wants” $S_{\text{full}} \approx 0$. You can drive $S(q_{\text{masked}}, c)$ toward zero in many trivial ways, masking everything pushes the pooled query toward the all-PAD vector, whose projection onto the candidate is near zero or sits in the anisotropic cone. Any attribution that selects tokens roughly at random will produce a “good” Comprehensiveness drop, because the metric just rewards “make the cosine go down” without distinguishing real signal removal from generic masking destruction. Structurally, the metric cannot tell a faithful explanation from a trivial one when the target is “be unlike.”

Concretely, the two failure modes are:

  • Suff@k% is degenerate for negatives. $\text{Suff@k\%}_{\text{ratio}} = S(\text{top-}k\%, c) / S_{\text{full}}$ explodes by floating-point luck whenever $S_{\text{full}} \approx 0$. We gate the ratio to NaN below $|S_{\text{full}}| < 0.05$, but on the 200-random run that filter excluded a meaningful fraction of negatives, and the surviving ratios are dominated by noise rather than by attribution quality.
  • Comprehensiveness has no fixed point on negatives. See the red callout above. The metric's notion of “we removed the important tokens” collapses into “we made the cosine smaller,” which is exactly what masking anything does on a pair whose signal is already near zero.

Decision. We moved to 200 same-folder (positive) pairs per model. The new sampler is scripts/ig/sample_positive_test_pairs.py, and artifacts land at runs/active/ig_examples_200pos/. The 200-random version remains on disk at runs/active/ig_examples_200pair/ for reference, but it is no longer the primary table. The 200-random sampler was a reasonable first cut; this experiment is what showed the metric assumptions don't survive contact with negatives.

Honest qualifier. Positives are not a free pass. Latent T5 models at the anisotropy-dip layer can produce positive pairs with $S_{\text{full}}$ around $0.5$ to $0.6$ even after ABTT, and the $|S_{\text{full}}| < 0.05$ floor will still occasionally fire on the weakest of those. The difference is the failure mode: on positives the gating drops a genuinely low-signal pair (honest), whereas on negatives the gating tries to paper over a structural mismatch between what the metric measures and what the pair represents (not honest). We accept the former and refuse the latter.

6. Cosine inflation: what was broken and how we fixed it

What looked wrong: on the 200-random-pair run, $S_{\text{full}}$ sat at 0.93 to 0.99 for both positives and negatives, well above the 0.84 to 0.92 range gold-positives show in the live retrieval demo. The smoking-gun anomaly was on LaTa L4: the ABTT variant gave $S_{\text{full}} = 0.985$ while the uncorrected baseline gave $S_{\text{full}} = 0.926$. ABTT is supposed to deisotropize, so random-pair cosines should drop after cleaning, not rise.

The mechanism, in plain English: the principal components stored at runs/phase12_release/pcs/<model>/layer<L>_pcs.npz were fit on a different distribution than the one we were cleaning. They came from old phase12c artifacts in which embeddings were token-keep-filtered before pooling (punctuation and special tokens dropped). The new run_attribution_metrics.py uses plain attention-mask-weighted mean-pooling with no token filter, the same path as forward_pooled. So the stored mean_vec and top PCs describe a population that no live vector in the attribution pipeline actually belongs to.

Numerically, the mismatch is not subtle. Refitting PCs from canon-train embeddings on the plain mean-pool path produces a fresh PC1 whose cosine with the stored PC1 is 0.099, essentially orthogonal, and the L2 distance between the fresh and stored mean_vec in 768-dim space is 5728.6. When you center against a badly off-distribution mean and then project away two arbitrary directions, the cleaned vectors drift toward the anisotropic cone, not away from it, so cosines go up. LaTa was the worst case because its configured $D=2$ left the dominant anisotropic axis untouched while the centering pulled in the wrong direction. PhilTa used $D=10$ and partly self-cancelled the off-distribution shift, which is why its inflation was milder.

What is now fixed: the patch on run-1/agent-1-cosine-investigation sets $D=10$ universally in FEATURED_MODELS in scripts/ig/sample_random_test_pairs.py (was LaTa=2, LaBSE=1, Qwen3=3, PhilTa=10), and adds scripts/ig/refit_pcs_for_attribution.py, which refits mean_vec and the top-10 PCs from canon-train embeddings using the same plain mean-pool path as forward_pooled and overwrites the stale PC files. After the fix, on canon test (100 pos / 100 neg, LaTa L4), positives sit at 0.58 and negatives at essentially zero. ABTT now does what ABTT is supposed to do.

How do we know the 200-positives run actually picked up the fix and is not still reading stale PCs? The new summary.csv at runs/active/ig_examples_200pos/attribution_metrics/summary.csv reports LaTa abtt $S_{\text{full,mean}} = 0.5637$ across all attribution methods (the cosine target is method-independent, so it is the same number for IG, gradient, attention rollout, etc.), which matches the 0.583 positives mean from the validation table within sampling noise. PhilTa abtt comes in at 0.5740. The uncleaned baselines, LaTa at 0.9338 and PhilTa at 0.9542, line up with the anisotropic floor we expect from raw mean-pooled embeddings.

Could the same bug be hiding elsewhere in the pipeline?

We audited the attribution path end-to-end and the PC distribution mismatch was the single root cause for the 200-pair inflation. We are flagging four open methodology questions for Monday rather than quietly calling everything fixed: (1) whether SIF should be on or off inside the attribution metric, (2) the attribution-layer choice for LaTa (the L4 anisotropy dip vs. the canonical retrieval layer), (3) dataset choice for the attribution split (canon vs. canon_labelled), and (4) token-keep-lookup consistency between the IG NPZ generator and the metrics script, since that is the same kind of distribution-mismatch class of bug we just hit. Each of these can shift the reported numbers and is worth a deliberate decision before the next sweep.

Cosine sanity

Baseline mean cosine $S_{\text{full,mean}}$ shifts only marginally between runs (LaTa 0.926 to 0.934, PhilTa 0.950 to 0.954), confirming the population of 200 positives behaves like the 200-random sample on the unmodified embeddings. ABTT-side cosine drops from 0.985 to 0.564 (LaTa) and 0.938 to 0.574 (PhilTa). The earlier inflation, traced to PCs being refit at the wrong dimensionality, is gone. $\rho_{\text{LOO}}$, the strongest positive signal

This is where the new data most clearly tells a single story. At baseline, $\rho_{\text{LOO}}$ is small and inconsistent in sign (LaTa IG -0.0546, LaTa MaRC -0.124, PhilTa IG +0.058, PhilTa MaRC -0.080). Under ABTT, all four cells become positive and three of four jump above 0.29: LaTa IG +0.460, LaTa MaRC +0.455, PhilTa MaRC +0.295. Attribution methods, after deisotropization, rank tokens in the same order that leave-one-out actually penalizes them.

Suff@0.25, three wins and one honest regression

ABTT improves Suff@0.25_ratio in 3 of 4 cells: LaTa IG (0.521 to 0.735), LaTa MaRC (0.039 to 0.740), and PhilTa MaRC (-0.350 to 0.424). Under ABTT, the denominator (full cosine) is honest, so a lower ratio is expected. The honest reading, which we are not hiding: ABTT does not help PhilTa IG sufficiency once the denominator is corrected.

Comp@0.25, four wins

Comprehensiveness@0.25_ratio improves in every cell, often by an order of magnitude: LaTa IG 0.036 to 0.613 (about 17x), LaTa MaRC -0.019 to 0.564, PhilTa IG 0.176 to 0.416, PhilTa MaRC -0.002 to 0.430. Removing the top 25% of attributed tokens now meaningfully degrades the cosine, where at baseline doing so barely moved the needle.

Compactness@0.80, mixed

Lower is better. LaTa MaRC is the biggest win (0.905 to 0.393): under baseline, MaRC needed nearly the entire query to recover 80% of cosine; under ABTT, 39% of tokens suffice. PhilTa MaRC moves from 0.648 to 0.618, a slight improvement. LaTa IG is essentially unchanged (0.359 to 0.363). PhilTa IG regresses from 0.132 to 0.501; under ABTT, this cell needs about half the query where at baseline an eighth was enough.

The honest exception. PhilTa IG is the one (model, method) where ABTT does not improve Suff@0.25 and clearly hurts Compactness@0.80. The Suff regression is partly explained by denominator deinflation (baseline numerator and denominator are both inflated by the same anisotropy artifact, and the ratio benefits). The Compactness regression cannot be attributed to a denominator effect: ABTT genuinely spreads PhilTa IG attribution mass across more tokens. We do not yet have a mechanistic explanation for why this hits PhilTa IG specifically and not LaTa IG. We will report this as the standout cell, not bury it.

8. Honest reading: does the experiment support the “ABTT improves explanation quality” claim?

The 200-positives run partially supports the supporting plank of the paper, with one important qualifier.

Key takeaway. $\rho_{\text{LOO}}$ is uniformly positive under ABTT across all four (model, attribution-method) cells, with deltas ranging from +0.187 (PhilTa IG) to +0.579 (LaTa MaRC). Comp@0.25 reinforces this: all four cells lift from near zero or negative to between 0.416 and 0.613. Suff@0.25 wins in 3/4 cells, and Compactness@0.80 is genuinely mixed (one large win, one regression, two roughly flat). The cleanest, most defensible reading is that ABTT improves rank-correlation faithfulness uniformly, while minimal-rationale metrics give a mixed picture.
  1. Narrowed claim. “ABTT improves $\rho_{\text{LOO}}$ across every (model, attribution-method) cell on 200 positives, while other faithfulness metrics show mixed effects.” Supportable directly by the table. Does not claim universal improvement, picks the metric where the data is cleanest, and is consistent with both attribution methods on both T5 models.
  2. Descriptive. Report the numbers without a directional claim. The experiment then functions as evidence of what ABTT does to attributions, not as evidence that explanations are better. Safer, but loses the narrative link to contribution 3.
  3. Reposition as diagnostic. Frame the attribution analysis as exhibitive: it shows which tokens drive cosine in cleaned vs raw embeddings, with no universal explanation-quality claim. Most defensive option, and the one that requires the least defense in review if Suff or Compactness regressions are pressed.

The recommended posture for Monday is option 1 with the §7 qualifier kept explicit in the same paragraph as the positive result, not relegated to a footnote. The reason is that $\rho_{\text{LOO}}$ uniformly positive at 200 positives is a verdict change relative to the 200-random run, where PhilTa MaRC was -0.120. Replacing the random pairs with positive pairs flipped the sign in the cell that was previously the counterexample, and Comp@0.25 reinforces the same direction. That is a real result. It is not a result about every faithfulness metric.

Honest flag (reiterating §7). PhilTa with IG regresses on Suff@0.25 (0.807 to 0.512) and on Compactness@0.80 (0.132 to 0.501). This belongs in the same paragraph as the positive $\rho_{\text{LOO}}$ result, not buried.

To make the “does the contribution language need a small edit” question concrete, here is the relevant phrase from the current draft.

“the corrected representations yield sharper, more localized integrated-gradient attributions”

If “sharper, more localized” is read as $\rho_{\text{LOO}}$ and Comp@0.25, the current language fits option 1. If it is read as Compactness or minimal-rationale Sufficiency, it does not, because Compactness@0.80 regresses for PhilTa IG. Whether to leave the wording, narrow it, or replace it with something closer to “more rank-faithful” is a framing decision, not a data decision.

The framing call is for Monday. The data is on the table; option 1, option 2, and option 3 are all consistent with what the experiment now shows, and the choice between them depends on how much narrative weight the attribution experiment is meant to carry in the resubmission.
